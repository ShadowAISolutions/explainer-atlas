Speed Up And You Will Go Slower

Fire your engine forwards and nothing happens where you are. Half an orbit later, on the far side of the planet, you are much higher up — and moving a great deal slower than when you started. Aim the burn by hand and watch the ellipse miss.

You are in a circular orbit 400 kilometres up, going 7.67 kilometres a second, and you want to be in geostationary orbit, 35,786 kilometres up. The intuition says point the nose outwards and thrust. Try it with the direction slider: tilting the burn outwards wastes almost all of it. The orbit you end up on is barely higher than the one you left, because the speed you added is pointing the wrong way to add energy efficiently.

Point the burn straight along your velocity instead and something strange happens. Where you are, nothing changes: you are at the same radius, just moving faster. But you are now on an ellipse whose low point is here and whose high point is on the exactly opposite side. A prograde burn raises the orbit half a revolution ahead of you, never underneath you.

Δv = √(μ/r₁) · [ √(2R/(1+R)) − 1 ] + √(μ/r₂) · [ 1 − √(2/(1+R)) ]

Why the speed goes down

Coast to that high point and check the speedometer. You left at 7.67 kilometres a second, added 2.40, and you are now doing 1.62. Everything you gained, and more, has been spent climbing. The energy of an orbit is −μ/2a, which rises as the orbit grows, while the speed at any particular radius is √(μ(2/r − 1/a)) — and out at 42,000 kilometres, a circular orbit only needs 3.07 kilometres a second to stay up. The second burn is not a brake. It is another 1.46 kilometres a second of forward thrust, needed to stop you falling straight back down to where you came from.

Two burns, and a third that sometimes helps

Hohmann's 1925 answer — burn prograde, coast half an ellipse, burn prograde again — is the cheapest transfer between two circular orbits using two impulses, at every ratio, and the page asserts that against a few thousand alternatives. What is true, and often garbled, is that a three-burn manoeuvre can beat it. Fly far past the target, circularise almost nowhere, and come back down: for ratios above 11.94 that bi-elliptic route costs less, because the middle burn happens where you are moving so slowly that changing your orbit is nearly free. The page finds that 11.9388 crossing numerically.

What to try

Leave the target at 6.23 and set the burn size to 0.9. The high point falls short by far more than ten per cent — near the target, a small change in speed makes a large change in apoapsis, which is exactly why the manoeuvre needs precision. Then set the direction to 30 degrees and put the size back to 1.0: the same amount of propellant, aimed only slightly off, and the orbit is wrong in both shape and reach. Finally take the target ratio to 20 and compare the total against the ideal figure; past about 15.6 the two-burn answer stops improving at all.
