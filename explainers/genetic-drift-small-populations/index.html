Both panels above are running the same experiment. An allele that gives its carriers a small edge starts at the same frequency in every replicate, and each generation the next lot of gene copies is drawn from the current lot with that edge applied. The only difference between left and right is how many individuals there are to draw from.

On the right, with fifty breeders, the paths drift a little and then climb. On the left, with six, they scatter. Plenty of them fall to zero and stay there, and the allele is gone from that population forever despite being the better one. Nothing went wrong in those runs. There were simply not enough copies for the edge to assert itself against the noise of who happened to breed.

One number decides it

u(p) = (1 − e−4Nsp) / (1 − e−4Ns)

That is Kimura's diffusion result for the probability that an allele starting at frequency p ends up in every copy. Look at what it depends on: not N, and not s, but their product. Double the population and halve the advantage and the curve does not move. The bottom panel draws this exactly — by solving the finite Markov chain rather than trusting the formula — and you can watch the two populations trade places by moving either slider.

When 4Ns is much less than one the exponentials collapse and u(p) → p: the allele's chance of taking over is just its current share, exactly as if it conferred no advantage at all. That is the drift regime, and it is not a vague statement. Set the selection coefficient so that 4Ns sits near 0.4 and the fixation probability is lifted just ten per cent above the neutral diagonal. Below that, selection is a rounding error.

Variation drains at a fixed rate

Drift is not only a randomiser, it is a one-way loss. With no selection at all, the expected heterozygosity — the chance two randomly drawn copies differ — falls by a factor of exactly 1 − 1/(2N) every single generation. Not approximately: that is an identity of the Wright-Fisher sampling step, and the selftest checks it to fifteen decimal places over a hundred generations. Six breeders lose about eight per cent of their remaining variation per generation. Fifty lose one per cent. That is why small populations become uniform, and why they do it fast.

The famous four-N figure needs its small print

Textbooks say a neutral allele takes about 4N generations to fix. That is true only for an allele that starts as a single copy, and only in the large-population limit. Start at half and the answer is 4N·ln 2, about 2.77N — a third quicker. Run the exact chain at N = 5 and even the single-copy case comes out at 3.50N, twelve per cent under the quoted figure. The formula is a diffusion approximation, and small populations are exactly where a diffusion approximation is at its worst.

What to try

Leave selection at 0.05 and drag the small population from six up towards the large one. Somewhere around twenty individuals the left panel stops looking like a lottery and starts looking like the right. That crossing point is where 4Ns passes one, and it moves if you change the selection coefficient — which is the whole point: population size and selection strength are not two separate things to weigh up, they are one quantity.

Then push the selection coefficient negative. A harmful allele still fixes sometimes, and the same curve tells you how often; starting from a half at 4Ns = −3, it still takes over about 18 per cent of the time. In a small enough population, being worse is not a disqualification.
