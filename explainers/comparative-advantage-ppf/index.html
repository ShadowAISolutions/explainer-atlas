The North workshop makes ten bolts of cloth a day, or eight barrels of wine. The South makes three bolts, or four barrels. North is faster at both, by a wide margin, and there is no reading of the numbers in which South is the better producer. Trade between them still leaves both with more than either can make alone.

The quantity that decides this is not output per day. It is what each workshop gives up to make one more bolt of cloth. North spends a tenth of a day on a bolt, and a tenth of a day would have made 0.8 barrels. South spends a third of a day, which would have made 1.33 barrels. Cloth is cheaper in North — not in days, in wine.

Where the gain comes from

Read the table the other way and South's advantage appears. A barrel costs North 1.25 bolts and costs South 0.75. Whoever is cheaper at cloth is dearer at wine, necessarily, because the two costs are reciprocals of each other. So there is always something each producer is comparatively cheap at — unless the two ratios happen to be identical, in which case there is nothing to gain and the joint frontier is a straight line.

cost of cloth, in wine = (wine per day) / (cloth per day)

Send every North day to cloth and every South day to wine and the world makes 1000 bolts and 400 barrels. Take that as the bundle the world wants — five bolts to two barrels — and ask what the two could manage without each other. North alone, making that same shape, gets 667 bolts and 267 barrels. South alone gets 231 and 92. Together that is 897 bolts and 359 barrels, against the 1000 and 400 they make by specialising. Trade is worth 11.43 per cent more of both, from the same days and the same skills.

The kink

The joint frontier — everything the two can make between them — is not one straight line. It runs out along the cheap producer's slope first, then bends to the dear one's. The bend sits exactly where the cheap producer runs out of days. That bend is the gain: a straight line through the same endpoints would lie strictly inside it everywhere in between, and a straight line is what you get when the two costs coincide.

The 11.43 per cent is the size of the prize; the exchange rate decides who gets it. Any rate strictly between the two costs — between 0.80 and 1.33 barrels a bolt — leaves both workshops better off than producing for themselves. At 0.80 exactly, North is selling cloth for precisely what cloth costs it, so North gains nothing and South takes the whole 44 per cent. At 1.33 it is the other way about. Halfway, at 1.07, North is 9.1 per cent better off and South 18.2. Push the rate outside the window and one of them walks away.

One assumption is doing work here: that the world wants the bundle full specialisation happens to produce. If it wants some other mix, one of the two has to keep a foot in both goods, and then its own cost is the only rate the market can clear at — which means it captures none of the gain at all. The window of rates exists precisely because demand lands on the corner.

What to try

Press Make the costs equal. Both panels keep their shapes, North stays visibly larger, and the gain drops to exactly zero: the joint frontier straightens and the consumption rings land back on the production dots. Absolute advantage is untouched and worth nothing.

From there, drag South's wine per day up by one, to five. The ratios separate again and the gain comes back, at 18.2 per cent. Then try a pure change of scale: set North to five and four, South to three and four, note the gain, and double all four numbers to ten, eight, six and eight. Both frontiers grow, every absolute advantage grows with them, and the gain lands on exactly the same 14.29 per cent, because not one opportunity cost moved.

Finally, drag terms of trade from one end to the other and watch the two green figures under the panels trade places. The world's 11.43 per cent never changes as you do it. The rate does not create the gain; it only says whose it is.
