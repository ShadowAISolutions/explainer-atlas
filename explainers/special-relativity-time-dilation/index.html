The Longer Way Round

Two identical clocks, one of them moving. The moving one ticks slower — not because anything is wrong with it, but because its light has further to travel and light refuses to hurry.

Build a clock out of nothing but two mirrors and one photon. The photon bounces up, bounces down, and each round trip is one tick. It is a terrible clock to own but a perfect one to think with, because its rate depends on exactly one thing: how far the light has to go.

Pythagoras does the whole job

Stand still and the photon goes straight up and straight back — a round trip of 2L, taking 2L/c. Now watch the same clock slide past you at speed v. In the time the photon takes to reach the top mirror, the mirror has moved sideways. The photon does not get to travel straight up any more; it has to travel along the hypotenuse of a triangle whose base is how far the clock moved.

(c t)² = L² + (v t)²

Solve for t and you have it:

t = L / √(c² − v²) = (L/c) · 1/√(1 − v²/c²)

That last factor is γ, and nothing in the derivation is deeper than a right-angled triangle. The only physics is the assumption you never questioned: that c in the moving case is the same c as in the stationary one. Drag the photon slider and the two triangles draw themselves.

Why you have never noticed

Pick someone walking from the menu. γ comes out as 1.000000000000 with the difference hiding around the twentieth decimal place. A passenger jet does no better. This is not because the effect is approximate at low speed — it is exact at every speed — but because γ − 1 grows like v²/2c², and c² is nine times ten to the sixteenth. You would have to hold a stopwatch for longer than the age of the universe to see a jet's clock drift by a second.

Then push the slider toward 1 and watch the curve go vertical. At 0.87c the moving clock runs at half speed; at 0.99c it is down to a seventh; at 0.999c a twenty-second. The blow-up is why nothing with mass reaches c, and why the last few percent of the slider costs more than the first ninety.

The muon that should not arrive

Choose the muon. Cosmic rays hitting the upper atmosphere produce muons about fifteen kilometres up, and a muon at rest decays with a half-life of 2.197 microseconds. Travelling at 0.98c it should manage 645 metres before decaying — nowhere near the ground. Detectors at sea level find them anyway, in large numbers.

The resolution is that 2.197 microseconds is the muon's own time. In our frame its clock is dilated by γ = 5.03, giving it 11.04 microseconds of our time and 3.24 kilometres of travel. From the muon's point of view nothing is dilated at all and it lives its usual 2.197 microseconds — but the fifteen kilometres of atmosphere is length-contracted by the same factor, so it has less distance to cross. The two descriptions are the same physics in different coordinates, and they agree on the only thing that is not a matter of perspective: whether the muon arrives.

What to try

Set the speed to 0.6 and read γ: exactly 1.25. Then 0.8, and read exactly 5/3. Those are not coincidences — 3-4-5 and 6-8-10 are Pythagorean triples, and the light-clock triangle is the same triangle. It is the cleanest hint that the factor is geometry rather than mechanics. The slider cannot land on √3/2 exactly, which is why 0.866 stops just short of 2 while 0.6 and 0.8 land dead on.

Finally, park the photon slider halfway and sweep the speed. The vertical path on the left never changes length; the diagonal on the right stretches without limit. The ratio of those two lengths is γ, measured with a ruler rather than computed with a formula — and the readout shows the two agreeing to the last digit.
