Three Ways To Spend A Bit, And The Floor None Of Them Reaches

Every extra bit you spend on a sample buys about six decibels of accuracy. There is a hard floor on what any coder can achieve at a given number of bits, and the interesting part is how close the obvious methods get to it.

All three are scaled to variance one, so the decibel figures are directly comparable.

Four lines climb across the upper panel and every one of them climbs at the same rate: about six decibels for each bit you agree to spend. Three of those lines are coders, doing the best they know how. The fourth is not a coder at all. It is a bound, and no amount of ingenuity moves it.

The bound comes from asking the question backwards. Instead of picking a quantiser and measuring its error, ask what the smallest possible error is for a source of a given shape at a given number of bits. For a Gaussian the answer is exact and short.

D(R) = variance x 2^(-2R) so R(D) = 0.5 log2(variance / D)

Doubling the number of bits squares the accuracy, which in decibels is the six per bit you can see in the gridlines. That is where every rule of thumb about bit depth comes from. What the rules of thumb leave out is how far from the bound real quantisers actually sit.

Clipping against coarseness

The first racer divides a range into equal steps. It has one decision to make: how wide that range should be. Drag the clipping range down and the steps get finer, but the tails of the source fall outside the last step and are reported as though they landed on its edge. Drag it up and nothing is clipped, but every step is coarser than it needed to be. The readout splits the error into those two parts, and the button jumps to the range where their sum is least. At four bits on a Gaussian that turns out to be about two and two thirds standard deviations, which is narrower than most people guess: it is worth mangling the rare large samples to sharpen the common small ones.

A number that belongs to a sine wave

Converter datasheets quote a signal to noise ratio of 6.02n + 1.76 decibels and it is easy to read that as what n bits are worth. It is not. Switch the source to uniform, tune the clipping range, and the uniform quantiser scores exactly 6.02n with nothing added: the selftest checks eight bit depths and the missing 1.76 is there at every one of them. That constant is 10 log10(3/2), the ratio between the power of a full-scale sine and the power of a uniform signal over the same peak range. It is a statement about what you fed the converter, not about the converter.

Putting the levels where the probability is

The second racer drops the equal spacing. Two conditions have to hold at the optimum: every reconstruction level must be the mean of the samples that map to it, and every decision boundary must sit halfway between its two levels. Alternating them converges, and the result is the Lloyd-Max quantiser. Watch the lower panel: its levels crowd where the density is tall and thin out in the tails. It is never beaten by the uniform racer at any clipping range — the selftest checks two hundred seeded source shapes — and on a flat source the two are the same quantiser, because when every region is equally likely there is nothing to redistribute.

And yet, as the bits pile up, Lloyd-Max settles about 4.35 decibels above the bound and stays there. Being the best possible quantiser of its kind is not the same as being good enough.

The last 1.53 decibels

The third racer goes back to equal steps but stops insisting that n bits means 2^n levels. It uses as many levels as it likes and pays the entropy of the result, so common cells cost less than a bit and rare ones cost more. That recovers most of the gap — nearly three decibels on a Gaussian, more than five on a Laplacian — and on a uniform source it recovers exactly nothing, because a flat source already spends every cell equally. What entropy coding buys is precisely the source's departure from flat.

Then it stops, 10 log10(pi e / 6) = 1.533 decibels above the bound, for every source, at every rate. That last gap is not a failure of coding. It is the price of treating samples one at a time: an interval is a clumsy shape to tile a space with, and the bound is only reachable by coding long blocks together, where the cells can be rounder. Every block coder ever built is an attempt on that decibel and a half.

What to try

Start at four bits on a Gaussian and drag the clipping range from one standard deviation to six, watching the amber line sag at both ends and the clipping share swing from almost all of the error to almost none. Press the tune button and note that the best range widens as you add bits, because finer steps make the tails relatively more expensive to throw away. Then switch to the uniform source: all three coders collapse onto one line, and the gap to the floor is exactly the 1.533 decibels that no scalar quantiser crosses. Finally try the Laplacian at one bit, where the entropy coder briefly has nothing to offer, and walk up to six to watch it pull away.
