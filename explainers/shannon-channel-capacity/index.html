The Line You Cannot Cross

Every noisy channel has a number. Below it you can send with arbitrarily few errors; above it you cannot, no matter how clever the code. Guess which side you are on before you look.

Set the crossover probability to 0.11 — about one bit in nine arrives flipped — and ask yourself how much of your message can survive. The instinct is that a channel this bad is nearly useless, or alternatively that with enough redundancy you can beat anything. Both are wrong, and the second one is wrong in an interesting way.

What the noise costs you

Each received bit carries some information about the bit that was sent, but not all of it, because you cannot tell a correct bit from a flipped one by looking. The uncertainty the channel injects per use is the binary entropy of the flip probability:

H(p) = −p log₂ p − (1−p) log₂ (1−p)

and the capacity of the channel is what is left of one bit after paying that:

C = 1 − H(p)

At p = 0.11 the entropy is almost exactly half a bit, so the capacity is almost exactly half a bit. You can push half a message bit through per channel use, reliably, forever — and you cannot push 0.51.

Why the curve is symmetric

Drag p past 0.5 and the capacity climbs back to one. A channel that flips ninety percent of your bits is exactly as good as one that flips ten percent, because you simply invert everything you receive. The only genuinely useless channel is the one at p = 0.5, where the output is independent of the input and carries no information at all. That is the single zero of the curve, and it is the reason the shape has a cusp there rather than a floor.

The part that is hard to believe

The obvious way to fight noise is repetition: send every bit three times and take the majority. At p = 0.11 that cuts the error rate from 11% to 3.4%. Eleven copies gets it to 0.05%. Keep going and the error goes to zero — but so does the rate, because you are spending eleven channel uses per message bit. Repetition buys reliability by paying rate, and in the limit it pays everything.

Shannon's theorem says this tradeoff is not fundamental. For any rate strictly below C, there exist codes with error probability as small as you like — the rate does not have to shrink at all. The rate stays at 0.49 and the errors go to zero together. What has to grow instead is the block length: you encode thousands of bits at a time rather than one, and the law of large numbers does the work that repetition was doing crudely.

And above C, nothing works. Not a longer block, not a smarter decoder, not more computing. The converse half of the theorem says the error probability is bounded away from zero for every code with rate above capacity. That is why the shaded region has a hard edge rather than a gradient.

What to try

Put the rate at 0.5 and walk the noise slider up from zero. Everything is fine until p reaches 0.11, and then the marker crosses the curve and the answer flips — one bit in nine is exactly the noise level that costs you half your channel. Then set the noise to 0.5 and try any rate at all above zero. Nothing is possible, which is the only completely honest thing a channel can say.

Finally, try p = 0.9 with rate 0.9. It works, and it should feel like cheating. It is not: a channel that lies almost all the time is a channel that tells the truth almost all the time, read upside down.
