Every introductory mechanics course arrives at the same tidy result: to throw something as far as possible, launch it at 45 degrees. The derivation takes two lines, the answer is a round number, and it is wrong for every object anyone has ever actually thrown. It is the answer for a projectile moving through nothing at all.

Put the two shots side by side and the difference is not subtle. At 40 metres per second and 45 degrees, a baseball in still air travels a little over half as far as the same baseball fired into a vacuum. It also lands differently. The blue parabola rises and falls as mirror images, and it strikes the ground at exactly the angle it left. The red trajectory leans: it climbs a long shallow arc, turns over, and comes down noticeably steeper than it went up. Nothing about the shape is symmetric, because nothing about the forces is.

Why the symmetry breaks

In a vacuum the only force is gravity, which is the same on the way up as on the way down, so the second half of the flight is the first half played backwards. Air resistance is not like that. It always opposes the motion, so it points down-and-backwards while the projectile is rising and up-and-backwards while it is falling. It is also quadratic in speed: double the speed and the force quadruples.

dv/dt = -g ŷ - β|v|v, β = g / vt2

The constant β is set here by the terminal speed vt, the speed at which drag exactly balances gravity, because that is the number people have an intuition for. A table-tennis ball reaches terminal speed in a couple of metres of fall; a shot put effectively never does. That single number is what the projectile menu chooses.

The projectile is fastest at launch, so drag is fiercest at launch, and it spends the first part of the flight being aggressively slowed. By the top of the arc it has lost much of its speed, so the descent is gentler, slower and shorter than the ascent. That is the asymmetry you can see.

Where the best angle goes

Now the interesting part. Switch on the angle sweep and look at the lower panel, which redraws the range for every launch angle from 3 to 87 degrees. The blue curve is the familiar v2sin(2θ)/g, a clean symmetric hump peaking at exactly 45 degrees. The red curve peaks to the left of it, and the gap widens as you make the projectile lighter or the launch faster.

The reason is that time spent in the air is expensive once air is charging for it. A high launch buys you a long flight, and in a vacuum a long flight is pure profit because the horizontal velocity never changes. Under drag, every extra second aloft is another second of horizontal speed being eaten. So the optimum shifts toward flatter shots, which get to the target before the air has finished taking its cut. Press the button to jump straight to the best angle and watch it fall to the low thirties for a tennis ball, and into the twenties for a table-tennis ball launched hard.

This is why real throwing sports do not coach 45 degrees. Javelin, shot put and long-range artillery all sit below it, and how far below depends on exactly how much air the object has to push out of its way.

What to try

Set the projectile to no air at all and confirm the two curves lie on top of each other and the sweep peaks at 45 — that is the sanity check that the simulation reduces correctly. Then step down the menu one projectile at a time at a fixed 45 degrees and watch the range collapse. Finally, hold the table-tennis ball and drag the launch speed from 10 to 90: the vacuum range grows as the square of speed, while the real range flattens out almost completely, because a table-tennis ball simply cannot be made to go much further by throwing it harder.
