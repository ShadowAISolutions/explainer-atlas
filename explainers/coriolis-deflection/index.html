Two maps, one shot. On the left there is no rotating planet at all: there is a puck sliding across frictionless ice in a straight line, and a compass rose painted on the ice that slowly turns. On the right the same flight is redrawn in the painted rose's own coordinates, and it bends. Nothing in the first picture pushes the puck sideways, and the second picture is just the first one seen from a turning chair.

How fast does the ground turn?

The planet spins about its axis at Ω, once per sidereal day. Stand at latitude φ and ask not how fast you are being carried around, but how fast the little patch of ground under your feet is turning about its own vertical. That is the component of the spin vector along the local vertical, and the geometry gives it immediately:

ωground = Ω sin φ     f = 2Ω sin φ

At the pole the vertical is the spin axis, so the ground turns a full circle a day. At the equator the vertical is at right angles to the axis: the patch of ground is tipping over, not turning, and a horizontal path over it never gets twisted. That is the whole reason the effect dies at the equator and reverses sign below it. The quantity f, twice the ground's turning rate, is the Coriolis parameter, and it is the only thing about latitude that the rest of the physics remembers.

The size of the miss

Aim at something a distance D = VT away. The puck arrives where the target used to be; the ground — and the target with it — has turned through Ω sin φ T in the meantime. The cross-track miss is therefore exactly D sin(Ω sin φ T), which for any flight short against a day is

miss ≈ ½ f V T²

Quadratic in time, which is why a rifle shot does not care and a weather system does. At 45° N, f is 1.03×10−4 s−1. The page opens on a puck at 30 m/s given a full hour: it covers 108 km and lands 19.9 km to the right, nearly a fifth of the range. Cut the flight to ten minutes and the same puck misses by only 557 m, a thirtieth of its range — the fraction missed is sin(Ω sin φ T) and depends on the clock alone, not on how fast you fired. Note what the formula does not contain either: the bearing. Fire north, south or west and the miss is the same size, always to the right in the northern hemisphere and always to the left in the southern.

The circle it would close, given long enough

Keep going and the veer keeps veering. A puck left alone on a rotating ocean traces a closed inertial circle of radius V/f with period 2π/f — 970 km and 16.9 hours for our 100 m/s puck at 45°. That period is exactly half a pendulum day, the 33.85 hours a Foucault pendulum takes to come back round at the same latitude, and the factor of two is the same 2 that sits in f. The faint dotted arc in the right-hand map is that circle. For a short flight it lies on top of the drawn track; stretch the flight time or shorten the day and the two separate, because the simple turning-ground picture is the first term of the exact answer, not the whole of it.

One honest caveat about the ice. A puck on a spinning planet also feels centrifugal force, and on this page it does not appear at all. That is not a cheat: a planet's own rotation bulges it into exactly the shape whose downhill pull cancels the centrifugal push, so a level surface on a rotating world is already the surface on which centrifugal force does nothing. What is left over is the term that depends on the puck's own velocity, and that term is Coriolis.

What to try

Start at the default and drag the latitude slider slowly down through zero. The curvature shrinks to nothing at the equator and comes back the other way below it. Now put the latitude back at 45°, pull the flight time down to 1800 s and then back up: the miss does not halve and double with it, it falls to a quarter and climbs back, because the law is quadratic. Finally drag the length of the day down to about two hours. The inertial circle shrinks to something the map can hold, the dotted arc and the drawn track in the right-hand pane come apart, and you can watch a puck that was fired due north end up travelling west.
