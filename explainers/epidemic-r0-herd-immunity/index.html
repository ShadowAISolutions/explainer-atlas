Set R₀ to 2.5, guess, and reveal. The herd immunity threshold is 60 per cent, and the epidemic infects about 89 per cent. Almost everyone guesses something close to the threshold, because the threshold is the number everybody has heard, and it is the wrong number to expect. Nearly a third of the population is infected after the point at which the epidemic was already in decline.

The threshold is a rate condition

One infectious person meets enough people to infect R₀ of them, but only the susceptible ones actually catch it. So the number of new cases each case produces is R₀ multiplied by the susceptible fraction s, and the epidemic grows exactly while that product exceeds one:

R_eff = R₀ · s growth stops when s = 1/R₀

Immunity in the remaining fraction is 1 − 1/R₀, and that is the whole derivation of the herd immunity threshold. It says one thing only: at that moment the number of people currently infectious begins to fall. It says nothing whatever about how many people are still infectious at that moment, and that is the entire problem.

Why it sails past

At the threshold the epidemic is at its peak. Every one of those people is still going to infect someone — fewer than one each on average, but not zero — and their contacts will infect someone too. The epidemic decays geometrically rather than stopping, and the sum of a decaying geometric series is not small. All those later infections land on top of a population that had already passed the threshold.

How far past is not a matter of opinion. Divide the equation for new infections by the equation for recoveries, integrate, and the time variable disappears, leaving a relation between where the epidemic starts and where it ends. Writing z for the fraction ever infected and s₀ for the fraction susceptible on day zero:

z = s₀ · ( 1 − exp(−R₀ · z) )

This is the final size equation. It is transcendental — there is no closed form for z — but it has exactly one positive root whenever R₀·s₀ exceeds one, and a few steps of bisection find it to machine precision. It contains no time, no population size and no detail about how long anybody is infectious. Two epidemics with the same R₀ and wildly different durations infect the same fraction of people.

The overshoot is not a rounding error

At R₀ = 1.5 the threshold is 33 per cent and the attack rate is 58 — the epidemic infects three quarters again as many people as it strictly needed to. At R₀ = 3 the threshold is 67 per cent and the attack rate is 94. The gap is widest just past R₀ = 2, where it reaches 29.8 percentage points, and it narrows at high R₀ only because the attack rate is running out of room below one hundred per cent.

The way to avoid paying it is to arrive at the threshold without an epidemic. Push the second slider up: vaccinating a fraction v before day zero starts the system at s₀ = 1 − v, and the epidemic only takes off if R₀·s₀ is greater than one. Cross 1 − 1/R₀ and the outbreak dies out from the seed rather than burning through. That is what a vaccination campaign buys: the same final immunity, with the overshoot removed.

What to try

Put R₀ at 2 and guess. The threshold is 50 per cent and the answer is 80. Then raise the immune fraction slowly and watch the attack rate collapse far faster than the immunity rises — at 30 per cent immune the epidemic infects another 36 per cent, at 40 per cent immune only 19, and at 50 per cent immune essentially nobody. The last few percentage points of coverage do most of the work, which is the opposite of the intuition that each vaccination protects one person.

Then set R₀ just below 1 and try to make something happen. Nothing does, at any starting seed. The peak of the curve is not a place where the epidemic is dangerous and below it safe; it is the place where the arithmetic changes sign.
