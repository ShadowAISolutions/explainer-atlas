Ten Degrees Doubles It, But Only Near Room Temperature

Warming a reaction by ten degrees is supposed to roughly double its rate. Push the temperature rail and watch what the rule is actually made of — an exponential not in temperature but in one over temperature, which is why the rule works at 300 kelvin and quietly fails at 500.

Set the rail to 300 kelvin and press plus ten. At the default barrier the rate meter goes up by a factor of 2.24. Press it again from 490 and the same ten degrees buys 1.36. Nothing about the reaction changed. The rule of thumb is not a property of chemistry, it is a property of where on the rail you happen to be standing.

What the law actually says

k = A exp(−Ea / RT)

The exponent is not proportional to temperature. It is proportional to one over temperature, and that single reciprocal is the whole story. A fixed step of ten kelvin changes 1/T by 10/(T(T+10)), which shrinks as the square of the temperature. At 300 kelvin that step is 1.08 × 10−4 per kelvin; at 500 it is 3.92 × 10−5, under four tenths as much. The exponential is being fed a smaller and smaller morsel.

k(T+10) / k(T) = exp( (Ea/R) × 10 / (T(T+10)) )

Two things set the size of the jump, and the folk rule silently fixes both. Across activation energies from 40 to 80 kilojoules per mole, the ten-degree ratio at 300 kelvin runs from 1.68 to 2.81 — a genuine doubling needs 53.60 kilojoules per mole exactly, which happens to be an ordinary value for an ordinary reaction, which is why the rule survives. Hold that barrier fixed and move along the rail instead and the ratio runs from 2.70 at 250 kelvin down to 1.29 at 500, a spread of 2.09 in itself.

Reading the barrier out of the data

Take logarithms and the reciprocal becomes an axis. Plotting ln k against 1/T turns the curve into a straight line of slope −Ea/R and intercept ln A, which is the only reason anyone ever measures a rate at six temperatures. Switch the plate over with the Arrhenius checkbox: with the noise at zero the six points recover the barrier to the last digit and the pre-exponential to fourteen significant figures, and the correlation is −1 rather than +1 because the slope runs downhill.

The same six points plotted against plain temperature are not straight at all: the best line through them misses by up to 0.30 in ln k, a factor of 1.35 in rate, while against 1/T the residuals are at the floor of double precision. That gap is the experimental evidence for the reciprocal, and it is why a chemist reaches for 1/T without thinking.

Now raise the noise. At one per cent on each rate the fitted barrier still lands within a fraction of a per cent, because six points across a hundred kelvin give a long lever arm on the slope. The pre-exponential is the fragile one — it is an extrapolation to infinite temperature, out past the end of the data, and a small error in the slope swings it by orders of magnitude.

What a catalyst does, and does not do

Drag the catalyst slider. The rate rises by exactly exp(ΔEa/RT), with no fitting and no approximation, because the pre-exponential is untouched. But watch the ten-degree meter while you drag: a 40 kilojoule catalyst at 300 kelvin pulls the jump from 2.24 down to 1.34. A catalysed reaction is not merely faster, it is markedly less fussy about temperature, which is exactly why living things run their chemistry on enzymes instead of on heat.

What to try

Put the barrier at 53.6 kilojoules per mole, stand at 300 kelvin, and confirm the meter reads 2.00. Then walk the rail up in ten-kelvin steps and watch a rule everybody learns as a constant slide steadily down. Switch to the Arrhenius plot, push the noise to 5 per cent, and see which of the two fitted numbers survives it.

Gas constant R = 8.314462618 J mol−1 K−1, exact under the 2019 SI. Rates are arbitrary units; only their ratios mean anything here. The synthetic measurements sit at six temperatures from 280 to 380 kelvin with seeded multiplicative noise, so the plate is reproducible.
