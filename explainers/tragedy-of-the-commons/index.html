Eight herders share a field. Between them they are grazing about thirty animals, and the field would earn more if they grazed seventeen. Nobody is being greedy or stupid. Every one of them has worked out, correctly, that adding one more animal makes them money, and every one of them is right, and the result is a field carrying nearly twice what it should.

Why the arithmetic does this

An animal on an empty common is worth a lot. Each extra animal takes a little off what every animal is worth, because they are all eating the same grass. So the value of an animal falls as the herd grows, and at some size the herd as a whole stops gaining. That size is the peak of the top chart, and it is where a single owner of the whole field would stop.

A herder who owns one animal in twenty does not see that peak. When they add an animal they take the whole of what it earns, and they carry only their own twentieth of the damage it does. The other nineteen twentieths land on their neighbours. So they keep adding long after the field has passed its best size, and they stop only when the animal earns nothing for them — not when it costs the field more than it makes.

value of an animal when X are grazing = a - bX one herder's profit = x ( a - bX ) - cx best for the field X* = (a - c) / 2b where it settles X# = n (a - c) / b(n + 1) overshoot X# / X* = 2n / (n + 1)

That last line is the whole parable as a number, and it does not mention the grass, the cost, or the price. With one herder the overshoot is exactly 1: sole ownership solves the problem completely. With two it is 1.33. With eight, 1.78. It climbs toward 2 and never passes it, so the common is never grazed more than twice as hard as it should be — which is either reassuring or not, depending on the field. The profit actually kept is worse news: it falls as 4n/(n+1)², so eight herders keep under forty per cent of what one would have made.

Nobody has to be shortsighted

Switch the top chart to what each animal earns. The line is still above zero well past the peak of the total, and that is the trap in one picture. Every herder standing at the settled herd size is making money on their last animal. Taking it off the field would cost them. They are not failing to see the future; they are seeing their own arithmetic clearly, and their own arithmetic is not the field's.

You can check this on yourself. Set everyone else to their share of the best total, so the field is exactly where it should be, and then look at your own curve. Your profit still rises if you add animals. The efficient outcome is not an equilibrium: it is a place everyone would rather be and nobody will stay.

Closing the gap

Charge for it. The damage one animal does to everyone else is real and it can be priced, and if the charge equals the damage, the herder's arithmetic becomes the field's arithmetic and they stop in the right place on their own. Press the button and the settled herd lands exactly on the peak. The charge that does it is (a - c)(n - 1) / 2n — zero for a single owner, rising toward half the value of the first animal as the crowd grows.

Two other things close it, and the chart shows why. Reduce the number of herders and the overshoot shrinks on its own, which is what enclosure did and what a fishing quota does by another route. Or let them agree among themselves: nothing in the arithmetic forbids seventeen animals, it only says no one will get there alone. Elinor Ostrom's work is a catalogue of commons where the people using them did exactly that, with rules of their own making, and kept the field.
