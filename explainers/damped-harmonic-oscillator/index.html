Pull a mass on a spring aside and let go. It comes back, overshoots, comes back again, and the swings shrink. Written down, that is one of the most reused equations in engineering:

ẍ + 2ζω₀ẋ + ω₀²x = 0

Two parameters. ω₀ is the natural frequency, set by stiffness over mass — it decides how fast everything happens and nothing else. All the interesting behaviour lives in ζ, the damping ratio, which is dimensionless: it compares how much the system loses per cycle against how much it stores.

Three regimes, one boundary

Try ζ = 0. The phase portrait closes into an ellipse and stays there: energy sloshes between the spring and the mass forever. Now nudge ζ up. The ellipse becomes a spiral, and the top trace grows a decay envelope of e^(−ζω₀t) — the peaks touch it exactly, which is what makes the envelope useful for measuring damping from a recording.

The oscillation frequency also drops. It is not ω₀ but

ω_d = ω₀√(1 − ζ²)

which is why light damping barely changes the pitch: at ζ = 0.1 the frequency is only half a percent low. Push ζ towards 1 and ω_d collapses to zero.

At exactly ζ = 1 the square root vanishes and the character of the solution changes. There is no frequency any more. The mass released from rest returns to zero and stops, without crossing. This is critical damping, and it is the fastest possible return to rest with no overshoot — which is why it is the target for a door closer, a pointer instrument, and a gun recoil mechanism.

Above ζ = 1 the system is overdamped. It still does not overshoot, but it is now slower. Set ζ = 2.5 and watch the trace crawl. This is the counterintuitive part: more damping past critical does not help. Two decaying exponentials appear, and the slower one dominates the tail.

Use the kick

Set ζ to 1.0 and leave the kick at zero: no overshoot, as promised. Now drag the kick to −6, shoving the mass back towards the wall as you release it. It crosses zero once. Critical damping guarantees at most one crossing, not none — the no-overshoot rule only holds for release from rest. A door closer tuned perfectly still slams if you throw the door.

Reading damping off a recording

Because the peaks ride the envelope, the ratio of successive peaks depends only on ζ. Taking a log gives the logarithmic decrement:

δ = ln(xₙ / xₙ₊₁) = 2πζ / √(1 − ζ²)

So you can measure damping from two peak heights on an oscilloscope trace, with no knowledge of the mass, the stiffness, or the force that started it. The quality factor Q = 1/(2ζ) is the same number wearing a different hat: it counts roughly how many radians of oscillation happen before the energy falls by a factor of e. A wine glass has a Q in the thousands. A car suspension is tuned near ζ = 0.3 — deliberately springy, because critical damping feels dead over a bump.

Everything on this page comes from the closed-form solution, and the trace is also integrated numerically with a fourth-order Runge–Kutta step. The two agree to twelve digits, which is one of the checks this page runs on itself.
