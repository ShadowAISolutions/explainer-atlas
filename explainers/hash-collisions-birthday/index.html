Four billion slots sounds like a lot of room. Hand a 32-bit hash seventy-seven thousand items and it is already a coin flip whether two of them share a slot. That is not a bad hash function; a perfectly uniform one behaves exactly this way. The reason is that collisions are not about items meeting slots, they are about items meeting each other, and a pile of k items contains k(k−1)/2 pairs.

Every pair gets an independent shot at landing together, with probability one in N. Once the number of pairs reaches the number of slots, you should expect a collision, and the number of pairs reaches N when k is around the square root of N. The square root is the whole story, and in a world where table sizes are powers of two, a square root is a halving of the exponent. A 64-bit digest gives you the collision resistance of a 32-bit counter.

Why the exact curve is a product

P(collision) = 1 − Π(1 − i/N) for i = 1..k−1 ≈ 1 − exp(−k(k−1)/2N)

Go the other way round and ask for the probability that everything misses. The first item lands anywhere. The second must avoid one occupied slot, which it does with probability (N−1)/N. The third must avoid two. Multiply the terms together and subtract from one. The exponential on the right is what you get by replacing each log(1−i/N) with −i/N. Its error is not uniform: it is three and a half per cent at sixty-four slots, one per cent at a thousand, and a tenth of a per cent at sixty-five thousand. That pattern is exact — the relative error shrinks as one over the square root of the table, so the same square root that governs the answer also governs how well the shortcut works.

Every table is the same table

The horizontal axis here is not a count of items. It is a count of square roots. Rescaled that way, the eight curves for eight table sizes lie on top of one another to within the width of the line, because the exponential above depends only on the ratio k/√N. At one square root exactly, the chance of a collision is 1−e−1/2, which is 39.3 per cent, whether the table holds a thousand slots or four billion. The fifty per cent crossing sits at 1.1774 square roots, a number that is √(2 ln 2) and has nothing to do with any particular table.

The collapse is not perfect at the small end. At sixty-four slots the crossing is six per cent above 1.1774√N, because the asymptotic argument throws away terms that only become negligible when the table is large. By one thousand slots the error is under one per cent, and by sixty-five thousand it is under a fifth of a per cent.

What to try

Put the table at three hundred and sixty-five slots and the items at 1.18 square roots: twenty-three people, fifty-one per cent, the birthday problem exactly. Now drag the table all the way to 232 without touching the second slider. The item count leaps from twenty-three to seventy-seven thousand and the probability does not move at all — the curve has simply been stretched along an axis you are not looking at. Finally push the items to three square roots and watch the measured squares below go almost entirely dark: at three square roots the chance of getting away with it is under two per cent, for every table on the slider.
