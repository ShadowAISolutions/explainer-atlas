The page opens on an already-sorted list of 120 numbers. The last-element pivot spends 7,140 comparisons on it. Median of three spends 600. That is not a constant factor you can shrug at — it is the difference between a sort that finishes and one that does not, and the only thing separating them is which element each one picks to partition around.

Where the quadratic comes from

Quicksort picks a pivot, compares every other element to it, and lands it in its final position with everything smaller to the left and everything larger to the right. Then it does the same to each side. Partitioning a stretch of k elements costs k − 1 comparisons, so the total cost is just the sum of the stretch lengths it ever partitions.

If the pivot lands in the middle every time, the stretches halve: n, then two of n/2, then four of n/4. There are about log₂ n rounds and each costs about n, which is the familiar n log n. If the pivot lands at one end every time, the stretches shrink by one: n, n − 1, n − 2, all the way down.

(n−1) + (n−2) + … + 2 + 1 = n(n−1)/2

That is the dashed curve on the chart: every pair of elements compared exactly once, which is the most a comparison sort can possibly do without repeating itself. Take the last element of a sorted list as your pivot and it is the largest element, so the partition puts it at the end and hands back a stretch one shorter than it started with. Every single time. Watch the depth column: it reads 119 for a list of 120, meaning the recursion went 119 frames deep instead of seven.

Nothing here is adversarial

It is tempting to file this under "worst cases are pathological input". It is not. Sorted and reverse-sorted both drive the last-element pivot to the exact ceiling of 7,140, and both are things you get handed constantly: rows already ordered by a database index, timestamps arriving in order, a list somebody sorted a moment ago for another reason. The input that actually needs the least work is the one the textbook algorithm handles worst.

What the fixes really buy

A random pivot gives up on the input's shape and takes its chances. Its expected comparison count is a known quantity, 2(n+1)Hn − 4n, which is about 819 at length 120 — a little under 1.2 n log₂ n, and it does not care what you feed it. Median of three looks at the first, middle and last elements and pivots on whichever of the three lies between the others. On sorted input that is exactly the true median, so the recursion is perfectly balanced and it beats the random pivot: 600 comparisons and a depth of 6.

But median of three is a heuristic, not a guarantee, and you can watch it lose. Choose the organ-pipe input, which climbs and then descends. The middle element is now the largest in the list, the first and last are the two smallest, so the median of the three is a near-minimum and the partition is badly lopsided: 1,383 comparisons against the random pivot's 709. Choose the eight-distinct-values input and the ordering between them scrambles again. There is no pivot rule that is best everywhere.

What to try

Press Show me the worst case and watch the amber curve climb away from the other two while they stay flattened against the bottom of the chart. Then drag the length slider and read the numbers rather than the shape: doubling the length from 120 to 240 takes the last-element pivot from 7,140 to 28,680, exactly four times as much, while median of three goes from 600 to 1,433, a little over double. That ratio — four against two — is what quadratic and linearithmic mean in practice.

Then switch to the shuffled input and drag the seed. The spread collapses: at length 120 the three land at roughly 880, 790 and 700 comparisons, within a quarter of each other rather than a factor of twelve, and which one wins changes from seed to seed. On genuinely random data the pivot rule barely matters, which is exactly why the problem goes unnoticed in testing and then arrives with production data that happens to be in order.
