Thirty-Three Degrees

Balance the sunlight a planet absorbs against the infrared it radiates and you get a temperature. For Earth that temperature is 255 kelvin — thirty-three degrees colder than the place you are sitting.

A planet in sunlight has exactly one way to get rid of energy: glow. It absorbs a disc's worth of sunlight and radiates from a whole sphere's worth of surface, and when those two rates match, its temperature stops changing. That is the entire model, and for a bare rock it is already accurate.

The factor of four

Sunlight arrives as a parallel beam, so a planet of radius r intercepts it across a disc of area πr². It radiates from its whole surface, 4πr². The ratio is four, and it is pure geometry — no physics in it at all. So the absorbed flux averaged over the planet is (1−α)S/4, where α is the albedo, the fraction reflected straight back without ever being absorbed.

Set that equal to the Stefan–Boltzmann law and solve:

σ T⁴ = (1 − α) S / 4 T = ( (1 − α) S / 4σ )^(1/4)

With S = 1361 W/m² and α = 0.30, that gives 254.6 K, or −18 °C. Drag the albedo slider and watch how little it moves: because temperature goes as the fourth root, doubling the absorbed energy buys you only nineteen percent more kelvin. The fourth root is why planets are stubborn.

One layer changes everything

Now put a thin layer above the surface that is transparent to sunlight but absorbs a fraction ε of the infrared passing through it. The layer warms up and radiates too — but it radiates from both faces, half up to space and half back down. That downward half is the greenhouse effect, and it is not a trick of insulation. It is an additional energy source arriving at the surface.

Write the balance at the layer and at the top of the atmosphere, and the whole system collapses to two clean results. The layer always sits at

T_layer = T_surface / 2^(1/4) ≈ T_surface / 1.1892

whatever ε is — slide the emissivity and watch the ratio in the diagram refuse to budge. And the surface sits at

T_surface = T_bare / (1 − ε/2)^(1/4)

At ε = 0 the layer does nothing and you recover the bare rock. At ε = 1 the layer is opaque and the surface is exactly 2^(1/4) times hotter — nineteen percent, about 48 K for Earth. Earth's real surface sits at 288 K, which this model reproduces at ε ≈ 0.78. That number is doing a lot of work: it stands in for water vapour, carbon dioxide, methane, clouds and the actual vertical structure of the atmosphere, all compressed into one dial.

What the single layer leaves out

Quite a lot, and it is worth being honest about it. There is no convection, so the model cannot produce a lapse rate and cannot tell you the temperature at any particular altitude. There is no wavelength dependence, so it cannot distinguish a gas that absorbs in a narrow band from one that absorbs everywhere. There is no latitude, no seasons, no ocean, and no ice that grows when it gets cold and reflects more sunlight, which is the feedback that makes the snowball state possible at all. What the model does get right is the sign, the rough size, and — importantly — the structural reason: the surface must radiate more than the planet loses, because part of what it radiates comes back.

What to try

Press No greenhouse and note 255 K. Press Earth today and note 288 K. The difference is the thirty-three degrees in the title, and every bit of it comes from that downward arrow.

Then set the solar flux to 2601, Venus's value, leaving emissivity at Earth's 0.78. You get about 340 K — nowhere near Venus's actual 737 K. Venus is not hot because it is close to the Sun; at its albedo of 0.77 it absorbs less sunlight per square metre than Earth does. It is hot because its ε is effectively pinned against one. Push the emissivity slider to the top with Venus's solar flux and albedo and you still fall short, which tells you a single layer is not enough — Venus needs dozens of them stacked, and that is exactly what its atmosphere is.
