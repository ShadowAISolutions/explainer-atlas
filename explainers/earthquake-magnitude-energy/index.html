Most people answer somewhere between ten and a hundred. The slider starts at a hundred, and the block on the right shows what a hundred magnitude 6 earthquakes look like against one magnitude 8: a tenth of it, a thin band along the bottom. The true answer is a thousand. If you guessed low, you are in the overwhelming majority, and the reason is worth more than the number.

Why a step is 32, not 10

The magnitude scale is logarithmic in ground motion. One step up is ten times the amplitude on the seismometer — that part almost everybody knows, and it is where the bad intuition comes from, because ten times the shaking sounds like ten times the earthquake. It is not. Energy does not go as amplitude; it goes as amplitude to the power of one and a half, because a larger earthquake ruptures a larger patch of fault and shakes for longer as well as harder.

energy in joules = 10^(1.5 M + 4.8)

So one step of magnitude is 10 to the power 1.5, which is 31.6227766 times the energy. Two steps are 10 cubed, which is exactly a thousand — no rounding, no approximation, just three halves times two. Three steps are 31,623. That clean factor of a thousand per two magnitudes is the single most useful fact on this page, because it lets you do the arithmetic in your head: every two steps, add three zeroes.

What the scale hides

A magnitude 6 releases about 63 terajoules, which is roughly the yield of the Hiroshima bomb. A magnitude 8 releases 63 petajoules, about 15 megatons. The 1960 Valdivia earthquake, at magnitude 9.5, released around 11 exajoules — some 2,700 megatons, more than every nuclear device ever detonated, and about 180,000 times the magnitude 6. Those three numbers are two digits apart on the scale and five orders of magnitude apart in energy.

A logarithmic scale is designed to make enormous ranges fit on one axis, and it succeeds so well that it flattens the range out of the reader's head along with the axis. The two squares on this page put the ratio back the way it is: both are the same size, one grain is one small earthquake, and you can see immediately whether your answer is in the right neighbourhood or off by a factor of ten.

Where the energy actually is

This is why earthquake energy budgets are dominated by a handful of events. Earthquakes follow the Gutenberg-Richter law: the number above any magnitude falls tenfold for each magnitude step, when the b value is one. So going up one step there are ten times fewer events, but each releases 31.6 times as much energy — and ten times fewer, each 31.6 times bigger, means the band as a whole releases 3.16 times more.

Sum that upward and the arithmetic is stark: the top magnitude band on its own carries 68.4 per cent of all the energy released by everything beneath it, which is exactly one minus ten to the minus a half. Over a catalogue running from magnitude 4 to magnitude 9, the largest tenth of events by count carries 99.3 per cent of the energy. The self-test integrates the catalogue numerically and checks it against that closed form. Counting earthquakes tells you almost nothing about how much energy a region has released; only the biggest few matter.

What to try

Reset, then set the gap to 3.5 without looking at the answer and guess again. The true figure is 177,828, and the grains become a fine haze you cannot count. Then hold the gap at 2.0 and drag the large magnitude from 4 to 9.5: the picture never changes at all, because the ratio depends only on the gap. A magnitude 4 is a thousand magnitude 2 events, in exactly the same way and for exactly the same reason that a 9 is a thousand 7s.
