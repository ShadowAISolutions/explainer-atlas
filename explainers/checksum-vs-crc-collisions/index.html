The message on screen is twelve characters, which is six sixteen-bit words, which is ninety-six bits. Two short tags travel with it. One is a checksum: add the words up and send the total. One is a CRC: divide the message, treated as a polynomial, and send the remainder. Both are sixteen bits wide, so both cost the same to carry. The page opens on two of the words swapped, which is where they part company.

Addition forgets the order

The Internet checksum is the ones-complement sum of the sixteen-bit words, the scheme every IP, UDP and TCP header has used since 1981. Addition is commutative, so the sum of the words does not depend on the order of the words. Swap any two of them and the checksum is bit-for-bit identical. There are fifteen ways to swap two of these six words; one of them is invisible to everything because the message happens to repeat a word, and of the fourteen that genuinely change the message, the Internet checksum catches none.

The second blind spot is quieter. Clear a bit in one word and set the same bit in another, and you have subtracted 2k and added it straight back. The total is unchanged, so the checksum is unchanged. Seventy-one such pairs exist in this message and the Internet checksum misses all seventy-one. Fletcher-16 plugs both holes by keeping a second running total of the running total, which makes position matter — and it still misses one of the fourteen swaps.

Division does not

A CRC treats the message as a polynomial over the field with two elements, multiplies it by xn, divides by a fixed generator of degree n, and sends the remainder. An error is itself a polynomial, E, added to the message; the receiver sees a difference only if the generator fails to divide E. So the question “which errors are missed” has an exact answer: the multiples of the generator.

E(x) undetected <=> G(x) divides E(x)

A burst of length L is a run of L bits somewhere in the message with the first and last flipped. As a polynomial it is xp·B(x) with B of degree less than L. The generator has a non-zero constant term, so it shares no factor with xp, and if L is at most n then B has lower degree than G and cannot be a multiple of it. Every burst up to the degree of the generator is caught, with no statistics involved. Push the length one past the degree and the guarantee becomes a proportion, and an exactly computable one: a fraction 2−(n−1) of those bursts slip through, and beyond that length the fraction settles at 2−n. On the strip at the bottom of the figure that shows up as a hard edge exactly where the generator’s degree is.

Degree is not the whole story

Two-bit errors expose the one thing degree does not decide. Bits flipped at positions i and j make the polynomial xi(1 + xd), so such an error is missed exactly when G divides 1 + xd — that is, when d is a multiple of the order of x modulo G. For CRC-16-CCITT that order is 32767, so no two-bit error inside four kilobytes is ever missed. For the degree-8 generator x8 + x2 + x + 1 it is only 127: flip two bits exactly 127 apart and the CRC reports a clean message. Halving the width cost you a factor of 258 in reach, which is why real standards pick their polynomials rather than picking any polynomial of the right size.

This is the case neither method was designed for and both do the same thing: a random error passes a code of n bits with probability 2−n, whatever the code. The difference between a checksum and a CRC is never the random case. It is the structured one, and structured damage is the kind software actually produces.
