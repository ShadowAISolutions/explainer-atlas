Two Fixed Ends Are Enough To Quantise A String

Drive a stretched wire at whatever frequency you like and almost nothing happens. Nothing in the equation of motion singles out the frequencies it does accept. The clamps do.

Sweep the drive frequency slowly from left to right and most of the journey is a flat refusal. The wire sits there. Then, over a span narrower than the slider's own step, it wakes up, throws itself into a shape, and settles back down again as you pass. Twelve times across the sweep, and nowhere else.

Nothing in the physics of a wave on a wire picks out those frequencies. A wave travels at whatever speed the tension and the mass give it, and a travelling wave is happy at any frequency at all. What is not happy at any frequency is the pair of clamps. Whatever the string does, the two ends must stay still, and a sinusoid that is zero at both ends has to fit a whole number of half wavelengths between them. That is the entire argument, and it is enough.

L = n x (wavelength / 2) so f_n = (n / 2L) x sqrt(tension / density)

A continuum with infinitely many ways to move has just been reduced to a numbered list. No quantum mechanics, no discreteness anywhere in the equation: a boundary condition did it. This is worth sitting with, because the same move is the whole of why a bound electron has levels and a free one does not.

Where you push decides what you can wake

Set the drive to the middle of the string and sweep again: half the peaks have vanished. The second mode has a still point exactly at the midpoint, so a force applied there does no work on it, at any amplitude, forever. The same is true of every even mode. Move the drive to a seventh of the way along and instead the seventh peak disappears, together with the fourteenth. Piano hammers are placed at about a seventh of the speaking length for this reason — the seventh partial is the sour one, and the cheapest way to silence it is to hit the string where it cannot hear you.

The rungs are not evenly spaced

The olive marks show where the modes really are, and out past the sixth they have drifted right of the whole numbers. Real wire does not only resist being stretched, it resists being bent, and bending a short wave costs far more than bending a long one. Add that to the equation and the mode shapes survive untouched — a sine is still zero and straight at a pinned end — while the frequencies stretch:

f_n = n f_0 x sqrt(1 + B n^2)

At the default stiffness the twelfth partial is already a fifth of a semitone sharp of twelve times the first; push the slider to 0.0004, about a mid-range piano string, and it is nearly fifty cents, half a semitone. This is why a piano is not tuned to arithmetic. Tune the octaves so the beats vanish and you are matching the upper string's fundamental to the lower one's sharp second partial, which lands the top of the instrument more than a semitone above where the numbers say it should be. Every concert grand on earth is out of tune with a calculator, on purpose.

Taking a shape apart

Because distinct modes are orthogonal, any starting shape can be written as a weighted list of them, and the weights are unique. Sampled at a finite number of points, that decomposition is exact to the last digit: the selftest takes a plucked triangle at sixty-four points apart and puts it back together to within ten to the minus fifteen. What is not exact is the curve between the samples. A pluck has a corner in it, and a corner needs every mode there is; twelve of them miss it by four per cent, and seven hundred and sixty-eight still miss it by a part in ten thousand. The error falls only like one over the number of modes, which is why a plucked note sounds bright the instant it is struck and dull a moment later, as the high modes that made the corner die away first.

What to try

Press the tune button, then drag the frequency by a single step and watch the answering column collapse — the peak is a few cents wide at this damping. Wind the damping up and the peaks broaden until they merge, which is a string that will ring at almost anything and hold a pitch at nothing. Set the stiffness to zero and watch every mode snap onto a whole number. Then put the drive at the midpoint and sweep: the even peaks are simply gone, and the string below shows you why, because every shape it will agree to hold now has an antinode under your finger rather than a node.
