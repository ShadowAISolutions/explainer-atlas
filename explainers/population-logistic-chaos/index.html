The rule is one line long. Take this year's population as a fraction of what the habitat can hold, call it x. Next year's is r times x times the room left over:

xₖ₊₁ = r · xₖ · (1 − xₖ)

Growth is proportional to how many there are, and to how much space is left. Nothing else happens. No noise, no immigration, no bad winters. Given r and a starting value, every future year is already determined.

Set r to 2.8. The population wanders for a decade and then locks onto a single number and stays there forever. That number is (r − 1)/r, the size at which births exactly cancel crowding. Move r to 2.5, to 1.8, to 1.2 — same story, different level. Below r = 1 the population simply dies out.

Where it starts to break

Push r past 3. The single value splits in two: the population alternates high, low, high, low, and never settles between them. The steady state has not vanished — it is still there, still a solution — but it has become repelling. Nudge the population off it and it leaves.

You can see exactly where that happens. Near the steady state the model multiplies any small deviation by r(1 − 2x*) = 2 − r each year. While that number is smaller than 1 in size, deviations shrink. At r = 3 it reaches −1 and deviations stop shrinking. The two-year cycle that takes over then has its own stability number, 4 + 2r − r², which hits −1 at r = 1 + √6 ≈ 3.4495. Two becomes four.

Then four becomes eight at about 3.5441, eight becomes sixteen at 3.5644, and the splits arrive so fast that they run out of room. The gaps between them shrink by very nearly the same factor every time:

δ = 4.669201…

Mitchell Feigenbaum found that ratio in 1975 and then found it again in other models with different equations. It is not a fact about rabbits. It belongs to the shape of the fold, not to the formula, and it shows up in dripping taps and driven circuits too.

Deterministic, and still unpredictable

At r ≈ 3.5699 the doublings finish and the orbit stops repeating at all. Set r to 3.9 and read the last two boxes. The Lyapunov exponent is positive, which means nearby populations pull apart by a constant factor every year; two starts a billionth apart are visibly different within sixty years.

That is the whole content of the word chaos here. It does not mean random: run it twice from the same number and you get the same sequence to the last digit. It does not mean complicated: the rule is one multiplication and one subtraction. It means that knowing the population to nine decimal places tells you nothing useful about it sixty years out — and no better measurement fixes that, it only buys a few more years.

What to try

Drag r very slowly from 3.54 to 3.58 and watch the lower chart. Then jump to 3.83: out of the smear, a clean three-year cycle appears from nowhere, and doubles into six and twelve as you push further. Finally set r to 4 exactly and try starting populations of 0.5 and 0.51. One of them collapses to zero in two years; the other never repeats.
