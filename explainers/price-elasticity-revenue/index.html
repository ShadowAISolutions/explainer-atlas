Put the price up by one per cent. Every unit you still sell earns one per cent more, and some buyers walk away. Elasticity is the second half of that sentence written as a number: the percentage change in quantity divided by the percentage change in price that caused it.

E = (dq/dp) times (p/q)

It is negative, because demand slopes down, so what people usually mean by elastic and inelastic is its size. Below one in size, the quantity gives way by less than the price rose, and the price rise wins. Above one, the quantity gives way by more, and the price rise loses.

Why the peak is exactly at one

Revenue is price times quantity, so differentiating the product gives the whole result in one line:

R = p q(p) dR/dp = q + p (dq/dp) = q (1 + E)

Quantity is positive wherever anyone is buying, so the derivative changes sign exactly when the bracket does, and the bracket is zero when E is minus one. That is not a property of any particular demand curve. Switch between the three on offer, drag the shape slider anywhere, and the peak of the lower chart lands on the elasticity readout showing minus one every time.

It also tells you what to do without finding the peak at all. Read the elasticity at the price you charge today. If it is smaller than one in size, you are on the left of the hill and a rise earns more. If it is bigger, you are on the right and a cut earns more. The readout marked a pound more buys is that derivative, in money.

The curve that has no peak

Choose constant elasticity and the hill disappears. If E is the same number everywhere, then either it is never minus one, in which case revenue climbs or falls across the entire range and there is nothing to find, or it is exactly minus one everywhere, in which case revenue is the same at every price and the chart is flat. Set the shape slider to fifty and look. This is the exception the rule needs: the peak is where elasticity passes through one, and a curve whose elasticity never passes through anything has nowhere to put it.

Cost moves the answer

Raise cost per unit above zero and the profit curve pulls away to the right of the revenue curve. Selling a unit for less than it cost is worse than not selling it, so the price that maximises what you keep is above the price that maximises what comes in. The condition becomes a markup rule: the gap between price and cost, as a share of price, equals one over the size of the elasticity. At an elasticity of minus two you charge half again as much as the unit cost you; at minus ten, a ninth more. Competition is elasticity, and this is the arithmetic of it.

Tick show what buyers keep and a third region appears above the price line. That is the value buyers got beyond what they paid, and it is largest when the price is lowest. The sum of it and your profit is largest at neither peak: it is largest when the price equals the cost, which is exactly the price at which you make nothing.

What to try

Leave the straight line and put the price at twenty, then at seventy. Revenue is nearly the same at both and elasticity is nowhere near the same; the peak is at forty, the midpoint of the choke price, which is where a straight line always puts it. Then set cost per unit to thirty and press both peak buttons in turn to see how far apart they have moved.
