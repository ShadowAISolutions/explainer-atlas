A polymeter is two patterns of different lengths sharing one pulse. Nothing is speeding up or slowing down; both wheels advance exactly one segment per beat. They simply run out of segments at different moments, and that is enough to make a pattern that seems to wander for a long time before resolving.

The length of the wander is the least common multiple. Three and four have no factor in common, so the pattern takes all twelve beats to come round. Four and six share a factor of two, so they agree every twelve — not every twenty-four. Six and eight, which look further apart, share a factor of two as well and also repeat in twenty-four rather than forty-eight. Pick two lengths with no common factor and you get the longest possible answer, their product.

repeat length = lcm(a, b) = a × b / gcd(a, b)

Why the downbeats meet exactly once

If each wheel strikes only its own first beat, the outer one sounds at beats 0, a, 2a, and so on, and the inner one at 0, b, 2b. A beat that belongs to both lists is a common multiple of the two lengths, and inside a single cycle the only one is the beginning. That is the whole feeling of a polymeter: one moment of agreement, then a long stretch of near misses. A common claim is that the two downbeats meet gcd(a, b) times per cycle. They meet once, always. The claim happens to be right for the 312 coprime pairs up to twenty-four, where the common factor is one, and wrong for the other 217, which the page asserts directly.

More than one strike per wheel

Turn the strike sliders up and the wheels fill in. The page spaces the strikes as evenly as the number of segments allows, which is what a Euclidean rhythm is: five strikes in eight segments comes out as the Cuban cinquillo, three in eight as the tresillo, and the same recipe produces patterns from West Africa, Bulgaria and Brazil depending on the numbers you feed it. The gap between consecutive strikes then only ever takes two values, differing by one, and the page checks that for every combination up to thirty-two segments.

With several strikes per wheel, agreements become common, and how many there are is decided by the common factor after all: two strikes coincide somewhere in the cycle exactly when their positions differ by a multiple of gcd(a, b). That rule, counted over every pair of strikes, matches a beat-by-beat simulation across all 8,464 combinations the page tries.

What to try

Start at three against four and drag the beat slider slowly. Now set both wheels to strike once and make them four and eight: the agreement is every eight beats and the whole thing feels like one metre, not two. Change eight to nine and the cycle jumps to thirty-six. Finally set fifteen and sixteen, the longest pair available here, and count two hundred and forty beats before anything repeats.
