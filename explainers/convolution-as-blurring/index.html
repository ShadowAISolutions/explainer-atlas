The Kernel Is The Whole Story

A convolution is a sliding weighted average, so everything it does is decided by its weights. Draw a signal, choose a set of weights, and watch the same operation blur, sharpen, or differentiate — then read which of those it will do straight off the kernel's own spectrum.

The six shapes differ only in their weights, and that is the only thing that differs about what they do.

The output in the top panel is built one sample at a time, and each one is a weighted average of the input around it. Slide a window across the data, multiply what is under it by a fixed list of weights, add the products, write the answer at the centre, move on. That is the whole operation. The list of weights is the kernel, and it is drawn beneath the signal as a row of stems so you can see the thing that is doing the work.

Because the weights are the only thing that varies, they are the only thing to look at. Switch from the bell to the derivative and nothing about the machinery changes — the same sliding sum now reports slope instead of a smoothed value, purely because half its weights went negative. The six kernels in the menu are all scaled to the same reach, so anything you see change between them is shape, not size.

Sign is the licence to overshoot

Turn on the range markers and drag the reach around with the box, triangle or bell selected. The output never once leaves the band. It cannot: if every weight is positive and the weights add to one, each output sample is a convex combination of input samples, and a convex combination of numbers sits between the smallest and the largest of them. No amount of blurring will ever invent a value that was not already bracketed by the data.

Now pick Sharpen. The output shoots past the band at every edge — the bright and dark fringes that a sharpening filter is famous for. Nothing subtle happened. The kernel has negative weights, the combination is no longer convex, and the guarantee is simply gone. Overshoot is not a flaw in the implementation; it is the arithmetic consequence of a minus sign, and the same minus sign is what makes the filter useful.

Read the ringing off the spectrum

gain(f) = Σⱼ k[j] · cos(2πf·(j − centre))

The bottom panel evaluates that sum at every frequency up to the Nyquist limit of half a cycle per sample. It is the honest summary of the kernel: a sine wave of frequency f goes in, the same sine wave comes out scaled by gain(f), and when the gain is negative it comes out inverted.

The box kernel's gain is the one to stare at. It hits exactly zero at every multiple of 1/N and then goes negative between the zeros, dipping to about −0.22 and never fully recovering. That is what ringing is. A box average does not merely fail to smooth the fine detail, it flips some of it over, which is why a boxcar blur leaves those faint reversed ghosts beside a hard edge.

The triangle cannot do that, and the reason is visible in the menu's own description. A triangle is a box convolved with a box, so its gain is a box's gain multiplied by itself — a square, and squares are never negative. Two crude averages in a row are better behaved than one, not because anything was cleverly designed but because squaring removes the sign. Push Applied how many times up with the box selected and the same thing happens in front of you: the gain is raised to that power, the negative lobes get stamped out in pairs, and the kernel drifts into a bell. That is the central limit theorem happening in a filter.

What to try

Set the signal to a single spike. The output is the kernel itself, which is the cleanest way to see that a convolution is just a sum of shifted, scaled copies of the kernel — one per input sample. Then switch to the step edge with the derivative kernel: the output is flat and near zero everywhere except at the edge, because the slope of a constant is nothing. Push the reach up and watch the peak widen and shrink, since spreading the measurement over more samples reports the average slope over a longer stretch.

Finally, look at the shaded margins. Inside them the window hangs off the end of the data and the missing samples are taken as zero, so those outputs are partly fiction. That is where the tidy conservation law breaks: the total of the full output equals the total of the input times the total of the weights, exactly, but the trimmed output you are looking at has lost whatever was pushed off the ends.
