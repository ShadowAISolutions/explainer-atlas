The River Cuts Its Own Corner

A bend eats its outer bank and builds its inner one, so it deepens and crawls downstream. Do that for three centuries and sooner or later two limbs of the same loop touch, the river takes the short way, and the loop is left behind as a lake.

The valley is four kilometres long and the river inside it is not. At year zero the channel is very nearly straight, with a wobble of a few tens of metres. Three hundred and twenty years later, at the settings the page opens with, there is ten and a quarter kilometres of river in that same four kilometres of valley, and eight abandoned loops lying beside it as lakes. Nothing was added: the same water, the same valley, the same 60 metres of width the whole way through.

Why the outside of a bend loses

Water going round a bend is pushed outward, and the surface tilts up against the outer bank. That tilt drives a slow return flow along the bed, from the outside back toward the inside, so a bend is really a corkscrew: fast water scouring the outer bank near the surface, and a sluggish bed current carrying the loosened sand back across to the inside. The outer bank retreats; the inner bank builds outward as a point bar at almost exactly the same rate. The channel therefore moves without getting any wider — which is why one width is the only length scale this page needs.

Curvature, remembered

The tempting law is that a bend migrates outward in proportion to how tight it is. That law is wrong in an interesting way: it makes bends swell in place and never travel, and it is unstable at short wavelengths, so the sharpest little kink always wins and the river ties itself in knots. The fix, due to Ikeda, Parker and Sawai and put in this form by Howard and Knutson, is that the flow arrives at a point carrying the memory of the curvature it has just come through. The migration rate is a weighted average of the curvature upstream:

m(s) = E · ∫0∞ κ(s − ξ) · (1/Λ) e−ξ/Λ dξ

The lag Λ does two things at once. It puts the fastest erosion slightly downstream of the tightest part of the bend, so the whole loop crawls downvalley, and it damps the short wavelengths that would otherwise run away, which picks out a meander wavelength of roughly ten to fourteen channel widths — the ratio real rivers show, from creeks to the Mississippi. Set the lag slider to its minimum and the short wavelengths are no longer damped: the channel goes spiky, passes sinuosity 6, and cuts itself off more than six hundred times in three centuries. Push it to 600 m and the opposite happens — the bends glide downvalley without deepening, and in three centuries not one loop closes.

Why the river gets longer

If the outer bank retreats exactly as fast as the inner bank advances, where does the extra ten kilometres come from? From the fact that the outside of a bend is longer than the inside. Erosion and deposition balance across the channel — the width never changes, which you can check directly: the channel's area is always its width times its centreline length, to the last decimal. But the same sideways motion sweeps more ground on the outer bank than it vacates on the inner one, and the difference is exactly the new channel length. Sinuosity, the channel length divided by the valley length, is the bookkeeping: it starts near 1 and climbs.

The correction

It cannot climb forever. When migration brings two limbs of the same loop within one channel width of each other, the bank between them fails and the river takes the short way across. Everything between those two points is abandoned in an afternoon, its ends silt up, and it becomes an oxbow lake. Watch the strip chart: the sinuosity climbs smoothly and then falls off a cliff. At the defaults the first cutoff comes in year 168, at a sinuosity of 1.55, and ten more follow over the next century and a half. Each one hands back a few hundred metres, so the curve saws upward instead of climbing cleanly, and by year 320 the channel is 10.1 km long in a 4 km valley. A meandering river is not drifting toward some final shape. It is held down by its own accidents.

What to try

Park the year slider just before a drop in the strip chart and step forward one year at a time with the arrow keys: you can watch the neck close and then see the loop detach. Next, take the erodibility to 5 m/yr and scrub again — the whole history compresses: the first cutoff lands in year 45 rather than 168, and the floodplain ends the run with over a hundred oxbows. Finally, narrow the channel to 20 m without touching anything else. Both length scales shrink together, so the river does not merely miniaturise: it moves fewer metres a year and its limbs must close to 20 m rather than 60 m before they join, and the two effects together drop the count over three centuries from eleven cutoffs to two.
