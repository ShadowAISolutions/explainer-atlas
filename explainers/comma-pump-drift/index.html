Sing It Round Again And You Are Flat

Take a choir through I – vi – ii – V and back, tuning every chord pure against the one before. They land a fifth of a semitone below where they started, and they will do it again every time round.

The first one is the comma pump. The second is not.

Pure intervals are small whole-number ratios. The other two shrink the fifth.

Four chords, each tuned honestly against the last. C major, then A minor holding the A the sopranos already have, then D minor holding its A, then G holding its D, then home to C. Nobody sings a bad interval. Every step is a ratio of small whole numbers, the kind a voice finds by itself when it locks onto a chord. And the C they land on is not the C they left.

Following the ratios

Track the root. C down a pure minor third to A is a factor of 5/6. A up a pure fourth to D is 4/3. D up a pure fourth to G is 4/3 again. G down a pure fifth back to C is 2/3. Multiply them and the answer is not 1.

5/6 × 4/3 × 4/3 × 2/3 = 160/162 = 80/81

The tonic has been multiplied by 80/81, which is one syntonic comma flat: 21.506 cents, about a fifth of a semitone, comfortably audible. Nothing has gone wrong. Each interval was exactly what it claimed to be. It is the round trip that does not close.

Push Times round up and the drift is strictly additive, because cents are logarithms: n times round is n commas, checked here out to twenty. After four cycles a choir has sunk 86 cents, most of a semitone, and after twenty they are more than a third of the way to the note below.

Two different commas

Switch the progression to twelve fifths and a second gap appears. Stack twelve pure fifths and you pass every note in the chromatic scale and arrive back at a C seven octaves up — except that (3/2)12 is 531441/524288 times 27, sharp by 23.460 cents. That is the Pythagorean comma, and it is a different number from the syntonic comma because it comes from a different journey. Just intonation fails to close both loops, and it fails by different amounts.

What tempering buys

Now leave the progression alone and change the tuning. Quarter-comma meantone shrinks every fifth to 696.578 cents — the fourth root of 5, chosen so that four of them stacked give a major third of 386.314 cents, which is exactly pure. The comma pump closes: the drift is zero, flat along the bottom of the lower plot. But twelve of those fifths now fall 41.059 cents short of seven octaves, and the gap has to go somewhere, so one fifth on the keyboard is left howling. That is the wolf.

Equal temperament takes the other bargain. Every fifth is 700 cents, two cents flat, which is one twelfth of the Pythagorean comma each. Both loops close exactly, and no interval except the octave is pure: the major third is 400 cents against a true 386.314, nearly fourteen cents sharp, which is why a piano chord has a shimmer a choir does not.

What to try

Run I – vi – ii – V in just intonation for ten cycles and read the drift. Then switch to I – IV – V, which closes perfectly in just intonation, and notice that the pump is a property of the route and not of pure tuning as such. Then go back to the pump and try each tuning in turn: the two tempered ones hold, and only the pure one walks away.
