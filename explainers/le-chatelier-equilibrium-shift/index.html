Two moles of nitrogen dioxide sit in a two litre flask at room temperature, mostly paired up as the colourless dimer. Halve the flask. Every concentration doubles on the spot, and the quotient that has to equal 216 drops to exactly half of it, because the quotient carries one more concentration on the bottom than on the top. The reaction is no longer at equilibrium and it runs forward until it is again.

Here is the part the slogan hides. The dioxide concentration before the squeeze was 0.047 molar. Doubling the density alone would have taken it to 0.094. After the shift it settles at 0.067: higher than it was, lower than it would have been. The opposition is real and it is partial, and the partial bit is the one statement here that is a theorem. Halve the volume of any gas equilibrium and the pressure rises by a factor strictly between one and two, never quite reaching the two that a mixture unable to react would give; over four hundred seeded mixtures the page measures that factor between 1.475 and 1.998.

What is not a theorem is the tidier version of the same sentence about concentrations. Give a reactant a big coefficient and a hard forward shift can consume it faster than the squeeze concentrates it. The page exhibits such a mixture and asserts the embarrassment: halving its volume takes one reactant from 0.0798 molar down to 0.0629, a fall of a fifth, while every other concentration in the flask goes up.

What the constant is a constant of

Q = product concentrations over reactant concentrations, each raised to its coefficient K = the one value of Q the reaction will sit still at, and it depends on temperature alone

Adding reactant lowers Q, so the reaction runs forward. Squeezing multiplies Q by a power of the volume, and the power is the difference in molecule counts between the two sides, so a squeeze pushes the reaction toward whichever side has fewer molecules. Neither of those touches K. Heating is different in kind: it does not disturb the mixture at all, it moves the target. Take the dimerisation to 400 K and K falls from 216 to 0.60, because the reaction gives out 57 kilojoules per mole and heat is effectively a product. The flask turns brown.

The same reaction, written backwards

Switch to the second preset. It is the identical chemistry, written as the dimer splitting, and now the constant is one over 216 and the side with fewer molecules is on the left. Squeeze it and it shifts left, which is the same physical shift as before under a different name. The rule is about the equation; the chemistry does not know which way you wrote it.

Two things that look like disturbances and are not

Choose the third preset, where two molecules go to two molecules. The volume slider now does nothing whatsoever: the volume cancels out of the quotient exactly, and the page asserts the extent of reaction is identical to the last decimal across the whole range. Then push the argon slider on any preset. The pressure readout climbs and nothing else on screen moves, and the assertion checks that the extent is not merely close but unchanged. Argon at fixed volume changes no concentration, and concentrations are all the quotient knows about. Add argon at fixed pressure instead and the flask must expand to make room, which is a volume change wearing a disguise, and the volume slider already shows you what that does.

What to try

Start on the dimerisation and push Extra reactant to one mole. The reaction eats 0.978 of the mole you added, leaving the dioxide concentration at 0.058 against the 0.047 it held before, so it partly undid the change and no more. Then drag the temperature from 298 K up to 400 K and watch the target itself slide down the strip while the bars chase it.
