Nothing on this page is approximated. Every point on the curve is the hydrogen ion concentration that satisfies charge balance, mass balance and the water equilibrium at once, found by bisection to the last digit a double can hold. That matters, because the whole subject is a stack of shortcuts and the only way to see what each one costs is to have the exact answer sitting next to it.

Add sodium hydroxide to a weak acid and the curve does three things: it rises steeply from the start, flattens into a long shallow plateau, then leaps. The plateau is the buffer region, and its midpoint is the first free gift. Half the acid has been converted, so half is still protonated, and the ratio of the two forms is one. Put that into the relation between pH and that ratio and the logarithm vanishes.

pH = pKa + log([A⁻] / [HA]) → pH = pKa when the two are equal

That identity is exact. Given the true concentrations of the two forms it holds to within four parts in a thousand trillion, which is the floor of the arithmetic and not a property of chemistry. What is not exact is the step everyone actually takes: assuming that adding half the equivalence volume of base leaves exactly half the acid deprotonated. It does not, because some of the acid dissociated on its own before any base arrived, and because water is contributing ions of its own.

What the shortcut costs

For acetic acid at a tenth molar the gap is half a thousandth of a pH unit and nobody will ever care. Wind the pKa down towards 2 and the concentration down towards a millimolar and the same shortcut is out by more than a whole pH unit: across three hundred seeded acids the worst case misreads a pKa of 2.30 as 3.41. A stronger weak acid dissociates substantially before the titration starts, so half the equivalence volume is nowhere near half deprotonated.

What actually governs the miss, though, is not the pKa but the concentration. Sweep the whole band from pKa 4 to 9 and the worst case is 0.176, and it sits at a millimolar; hold the acid at a twentieth molar or stronger and the same sweep never exceeds 0.005. That is why the shortcut survives in the textbooks. Dilution is what breaks it, because the more dilute the acid the larger a share of it has already dissociated before the burette is opened.

The equivalence point is not at pH 7

At equivalence every acid molecule has been converted to its conjugate base, and that base pulls a proton off water. The solution ends up basic, always: over three hundred acids the lowest equivalence pH found was 7.027, and the weaker the acid the further above seven it sits. Only a strong acid lands exactly on seven, and then it lands there exactly — at equivalence nothing is left but water and a spectator salt, so the hydrogen ion concentration is the square root of the water constant and the pH is 7.0000000000.

The usual estimate for the weak case treats the conjugate base as if it were alone in solution and ignores the acid it came from. That is worth four decimal places at ordinary concentrations and rather less elsewhere: the worst of the same three hundred acids has the formula predicting 6.564 where the real answer is 7.027, an error of 0.463 — and on the wrong side of neutral, which is the one thing the estimate was supposed to settle.

What to try

Press the button that jumps to half equivalence and watch the readout beside the pKa: at the default acid they agree to two decimals. Now drag the pKa down to 2 and the concentration down to a millimolar and watch the same two numbers separate by a whole unit while the plateau itself disappears — there is no flat region left to have a midpoint. Then switch to hydrochloric acid and jump to equivalence: the leap becomes a cliff and the pH lands on 7 exactly. Finally set the base concentration to ten times the acid and notice that the equivalence volume moves but neither landmark does, because both are properties of the acid rather than of the burette.
