Building a Corner Out of Curves

Sine waves are smooth and endless. A square wave is flat, then instantly somewhere else. Add enough of the first and you get the second — almost. The gap that remains is the interesting part.

Start with one harmonic. A single sine wave is a poor square wave, but it has the right period and it is going the right way at the right times. Add a third harmonic at a third of the amplitude and the top begins to flatten. Add a fifth, a seventh, a ninth. By eight terms the flat sections are convincing and the edges are steep. By forty the picture looks, at a glance, like a square wave.

Look closely at the edge. There is a spike there — a little ear that pokes above the flat top just before the jump. Push the harmonic count from eight to sixty-four. The ear gets narrower. It slides closer to the jump. It does not get shorter.

Why these particular amplitudes

The square wave that swings between −1 and +1 is odd about the origin, so only sines appear, and it is symmetric in a way that kills every even harmonic. What survives is

f(x) = (4/π) [ sin x + (1/3) sin 3x + (1/5) sin 5x + … ]

Each coefficient is the average of the wave against that sine over one period, doubled. For the square wave that integral is easy, and it gives 4/(nπ) for odd n and exactly zero for even n. The sawtooth keeps every harmonic but alternates their sign, falling off like 1/n. The triangle keeps only odd harmonics and falls off like 1/n² — much faster, which is why it locks onto its target so quickly.

That rate of falloff is not arbitrary. It reads off the smoothness of the wave you are building. A wave with a jump needs coefficients that die like 1/n; a wave that is continuous but has a corner gets 1/n²; a wave smooth all the way down would have coefficients falling faster than any power. Roughness is expensive, and the bill is paid in high harmonics.

The overshoot that never leaves

Set the target to square and put the harmonic count at 64. The peak of the sum sits at about 1.1790, roughly nine percent above the target value of 1. Now try 50, 20, 10. The peak barely moves. It is converging — but to 1.1790, not to 1.

That number is not a numerical accident. The peak of the sum of the first N terms sits exactly at x = π/(2N), and evaluating the sum there turns it into a midpoint rule for an integral. As N grows the rule becomes exact and the peak tends to

(2/π) ∫0π (sin t)/t dt = 1.1789797…

This is the Gibbs phenomenon. The series does converge to the square wave at every single point away from the jump — pick any such point and the sum will get there — but the worst error over the whole period does not shrink. The overshoot only retreats sideways, into an ever-thinner sliver next to the discontinuity. Its height is fixed and its width goes to zero, which is exactly how something can converge pointwise and still look permanently wrong.

What to try

Switch to the triangle wave and sweep the harmonic count. There is no ear at all, and the highest point of the sum stays below 1 and climbs toward it. No jump, no Gibbs. Then switch to the sawtooth: the ear returns, at the same nine percent, because the size of the overshoot depends only on the size of the jump.

Turn on Show each harmonic at four or five terms. The individual sines all cross zero together at the jump, which is why the sum is pinned to exactly zero there no matter how many you add — the series converges to the midpoint of a jump, not to either side of it. Finally, watch the energy readout: at eight harmonics the square wave already has over 97 percent of its energy, which is why it looks nearly right long before it is.
