The Law Says Who Pays, the Market Decides Who Bears It

Before the tax, the market clears at a price of 10 and a quantity of 100. Introduce a tax of t per unit and two prices appear where there was one: the price a buyer hands over, and the smaller amount a seller is left holding. The gap between them is exactly t, by definition, and the quantity traded falls until supply and demand agree on that gap.

The split is arithmetic, not politics

Let Ed be the elasticity of demand and Es the elasticity of supply — the percentage change in quantity per percentage change in price, taken as positive numbers. Solving the two curves with a wedge of t between them gives the rise in the buyer's price directly:

buyers' share = Es / (Es + Ed)
sellers' share = Ed / (Es + Ed)

Read that carefully: your share of the burden is governed by the other side's elasticity. Whoever can more easily walk away pays less. If buyers are unresponsive and sellers are flexible, the tax lands almost entirely on buyers, because sellers simply reduce output until the price rises to cover it. Reverse the elasticities and the burden reverses with them.

Set both sliders to 0 on demand and watch: the buyers' share goes to 100%, the quantity does not move at all, and the tax raises the maximum possible revenue with no lost trade. That is the theoretical case for taxing things people will buy regardless — and the practical reason those taxes feel so unavoidable.

Why the statutory side is irrelevant

Change the last control from sellers to buyers. Every number on the page stays exactly where it was. The algebra is the same either way, because the market only ever responds to the two prices, and the law fixes only the distance between them, not where that gap sits.

This is not a modelling trick; it is the definition of the equilibrium. A payroll tax split half and half between employer and employee is, in economic terms, the same tax as one levied wholly on either side, and it is borne in whatever proportion the elasticities of labour supply and labour demand dictate. The line on the payslip records an accounting convention.

What the triangle costs

Some trades were worth doing before the tax and are not worth doing with it. Those trades do not move elsewhere; they stop. The value they would have created is the triangle to the right of the new quantity:

deadweight loss = ½ · t · (fall in quantity)

Because the fall in quantity is itself proportional to t, the loss grows with the square of the tax: double the rate and the waste quadruples, while the revenue does not. That asymmetry is why a broad low tax on many things generally destroys less value than a steep one on a few, and why the loss is smallest exactly where the quantity moves least.

What to try

Put the tax at 2 and set demand elasticity to 0.2 with supply at 3. The buyers carry about 94% of it. Now swap the two numbers and the sellers carry the same 94%. Nothing about the tax changed — only who could more easily do without the trade.
