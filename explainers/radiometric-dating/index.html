The Clock Does Not Care How Much You Started With

You cannot weigh a rock and learn its age. You can count what has turned into what, and that ratio is the same whether you picked up a gram or a tonne.

Each has its own half-life, and that is the only thing that changes between them.

Drag the third slider, the one labelled how much you collected, from 5 to 500 and watch the age readout. It does not move. A hundred times more material decays a hundred times faster in absolute terms, and the fraction left behind is identical, which is the whole reason radiometric dating works on a chip of zircon the size of a full stop.

Why a fraction and not an amount

Each atom of the parent isotope has a fixed probability of decaying in the next second, unaffected by temperature, pressure, chemistry or what its neighbours are doing. So the number decaying per second is proportional to how many are left, which gives an exponential:

N(t) = N₀ · 2^(−t / T) equivalently N₀ · e^(−λt), λT = ln 2

Nothing survives that is not a ratio. Divide N(t) by N₀ and the starting amount is gone, and since every parent atom that leaves becomes exactly one daughter atom, the daughter count D is N₀ − N. Rearranging for the age gives an expression made entirely of things you can measure today:

t = T · log₂( 1 + D/N )

You never need to know N₀. That is the trick, and it is the only trick, and everything else in the subject is about the conditions under which it holds.

Four clocks with wildly different reaches

Switch the isotope system. Carbon-14 has a half-life of 5730 years, so after ten half-lives — 57,300 years — a thousandth of the original remains and the measurement runs out of signal. That is the ceiling on radiocarbon, and it is a property of the isotope, not of the instrument. Uranium-238 takes 4.468 billion years to halve, which is why zircons carrying it date the oldest rocks on Earth but are useless on a Roman coin: after two thousand years the ratio has barely left zero.

Notice what does not change: the shape on the upper chart is exactly the same curve for all four. Only the axis labels differ. A clock is a clock; picking one is entirely about matching its half-life to the interval you are trying to measure.

The assumption that breaks first

The formula above assumed every daughter atom got there by decay. Push the fourth slider up and watch the raw-ratio age run away from the true age — it reads far too old, because it is crediting inherited daughter to a decay that never happened. Argon-argon and rubidium-strontium dating live with this constantly; rocks do not form chemically pure.

The isochron on the lower chart fixes it without ever knowing the initial amount. Take several minerals from the same rock, which crystallised at the same moment but locked in different parent concentrations. Divide everything by a stable isotope of the daughter element, which does not decay and is not produced, and each mineral plots as a point satisfying

D/Dₛ = (D/Dₛ)₀ + (P/Dₛ) · (e^(λt) − 1)

That is a straight line. Its intercept is the inherited daughter, which the fit hands you for free instead of requiring you to assume it, and its slope is e^(λt) − 1, so the age is ln(1 + slope)/λ. Move the daughter slider and the whole line slides up the y-axis while its slope, and therefore the isochron age, does not budge.

What to try

Set potassium-argon, put the age at 2 half-lives and the initial daughter at 0. Both age readouts agree at 2.50 billion years. Now push the initial daughter to 1.00: the raw ratio climbs to 3.29 billion years, almost a third too old, while the isochron stays exactly where it was. That gap is the difference between a date you can publish and a date that is an artefact of your assumption.

Then take the age slider down below about a tenth of a half-life on any system. The isochron points crowd towards a horizontal line, the slope becomes tiny, and small scatter in the measurements swamps it. That is the other end of the range: a clock cannot read a time much shorter than the interval it ticks in.
