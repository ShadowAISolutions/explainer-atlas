Two Antennas, One Beam, And The Formula That Loses Half Of It

An array of two elements has a pattern set entirely by how far apart they sit and how their phases differ. The standard main-lobe formula tells you where one beam points. Often there are two.

Two identical elements, a distance d apart, driven with a phase offset φ. That is the whole machine. Everything the pair radiates in a given direction is the sum of two waves that arrived by paths of different length, so the only thing that matters is how far out of step they are when they get there.

Call the angle from the array axis θ. The extra path the far element's wave travels is d cos θ, which costs it kd cos θ radians of phase, where k = 2π/λ. Add the driving offset and the two waves differ by ψ = kd cos θ + φ. Summing two unit phasors that far apart gives the array factor.

|AF(θ)| = |1 + eiψ| = 2 |cos(ψ/2)|,   ψ = kd cos θ + φ

The absolute value is not decoration. Written without it, the expression goes negative half the time, and a negative amplitude is not a direction you can point an antenna at. The page's first test sums the two phasors numerically at forty-four thousand angles and checks the closed form against them; they agree to 4.4×10−16, which is as close as double precision gets.

Where the beam points

A full-amplitude lobe needs cos(ψ/2) = ±1, so ψ must be an even multiple of π. Solve for the angle and you get the formula every textbook prints: θ = arccos(−φ/kd). Set the spacing to half a wavelength and the phase to zero and it says 90°, broadside, which is right. Set them to a quarter wavelength and −90° and it says 0°, straight down the axis, which is also right.

Now press Two beams. Half a wavelength, opposite phase. The formula returns 180.000000000° and the array is in fact radiating just as hard at 0.000000000°, in exactly the opposite direction, which the formula never mentions. It cannot: arccos returns one value, and ψ = 2πm has a solution for every integer m whose angle stays inside the visible range. Over two thousand seeded settings of spacing and phase, 502 have a single lobe, 1255 have two or more, and 243 have none at all — the pair radiates everywhere, just never at full strength. At the worst of them a real lobe sits 143.7° away from where the formula pointed.

Counting the nulls

Nulls need cos(ψ/2) = 0, so ψ must be an odd multiple of π. Sweeping only the spacing makes it look as though the count follows from d alone, and it does not. At half a wavelength the array has two nulls at 0° phase, one at 90°, one at 180°. At a full wavelength the same three phases give two, two and three. The count is a closed form in both quantities at once, the number of odd multiples of π that ψ passes through as cos θ runs from −1 to 1, and in that form it agrees with a direct count in all three thousand sampled settings.

What the whole thing radiates

Integrate the power pattern over the sphere and the interference term collapses into a single sinc: the total comes out 4(1 + sinc(kd) cos φ) times the power one element would send, and the directivity is 2/(1 + sinc(kd) cos φ). Both are checked here against Simpson integration and agree to about 10−13. Slide the spacing to zero with the phases matched and the directivity falls to 1: two elements on top of each other, in step, are one element. Push to half a wavelength broadside and it is exactly 2 — the same two watts, aimed into half the sky.

What to try

Start at Broadside and drag the phase slowly to 180°, watching the single sideways beam split and swing out to the two ends. Then set the spacing to 1.5λ at 0° phase and count the magenta arrows: three lobes and four nulls, and only one of those lobes is the one the dashed line knows about. Switch the vertical scale to decibels to see how sharp the nulls really are, and watch the readout marked what acos(−φ/kd) says go blank whenever the formula's argument leaves −1 to 1 while the array is still plainly radiating.
