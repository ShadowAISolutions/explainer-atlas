The Delay You Cannot Tune Away

A controller can be tuned out of almost anything: a sluggish valve, a heavy tank, a weak actuator. It cannot be tuned out of a delay between acting and seeing the result. Past one particular gain, set by the delay alone, the loop oscillates whatever you do to it.

Turn the gain up. At first the loop gets better: it reaches the grey line sooner and sits closer to it. Then it overshoots, then it rings, and somewhere near four and a half it stops settling at all and swings forever. Nothing about the controller changed except one number, and nothing about the plant changed at all. The ceiling you just hit belongs to the dead time.

Dead time and lag both make a plant slow, and they are not the same slowness. A lag responds immediately and weakly: push the input and the output starts moving on the same instant, just gently. Dead time responds not at all and then fully: push the input and for four tenths of a second the output does not know you exist. Drag lag from 1 to its top and the ceiling rises from 4.59 to 10.46; drag dead time to its top instead and the ceiling falls to 1.52. Drag dead time all the way to zero and the ceiling does not merely rise, it stops existing: the loop is stable at every gain there is, no matter how sluggish the lag.

Where the ceiling comes from

Feedback works by acting on the error it can see. Every lag in the path means the error it can see is out of date, and out-of-date corrections arrive pointing the wrong way. At one particular frequency the round trip through the plant delays a wave by exactly half a cycle, so a correction that left as a push comes back as a pull. Whether that matters depends only on how big the wave is when it gets back. For a first order plant with a delay, that frequency solves

delay × w + arctan(w × lag) = pi

and the gain at which the returning wave is exactly as large as the one that left is

critical gain = sqrt(1 + (w × lag)^2)

Below it the wave shrinks on every trip round and the ringing dies. Above it the wave grows on every trip and the ringing does not. There is no tuning of a proportional controller that escapes this, because the gain is the whole of the tuning.

Doubling the delay does not halve the ceiling

It is tempting, and it is wrong. Double the dead time and the offending frequency does not halve: the arctangent term takes up part of the budget, and it gives some of that part back as the frequency falls. The ceiling therefore always drops by less than half. At the defaults, doubling the dead time from 0.4 to 0.8 takes the critical gain from 4.59 to 2.64, a factor of 0.577, not 0.5. The shorter the delay the closer the factor creeps toward a half, and it never arrives. With a very short delay the ceiling approaches pi × lag / (2 × delay), which is the only regime where halving is nearly right.

Phase margin is not damping here

The tuning rule everyone is taught says the damping ratio is roughly the phase margin in degrees divided by a hundred: sixty degrees of margin buys a damping of about 0.6. That rule is about a second order system with no delay, and this loop is not one. Dead time makes the phase fall in a straight line forever, so a loop can show a comfortable phase margin at the crossover frequency and still be nearly undamped at the frequency that actually rings. Set dead time to 2, lag to 0.75 and gain to 1.20: the margin readout says 45 degrees, which the rule reads as a damping of 0.45, and the mode that rings has a damping of 0.04. The margin is real. What it is worth is not what the rule says.

The error you are left holding

A proportional controller only pushes when there is an error, so it can never quite arrive: the output settles at gain / (1 + gain) and leaves 1 / (1 + gain) on the table. Wanting that gap small is exactly what makes you want high gain, and the dead time is what forbids it. That tension is the whole of process control. An integral term removes the offset without raising the gain, which is why it exists, and it costs phase of its own, which is why it is never free.

What to try

Press tune to the best match at the default delay: the best gain is 2.71 and the match is 72 per cent. Drag dead time to 1.2 and press it again: 1.40 and 55 per cent. The tuning is optimal both times and the result is worse, because the ceiling came down. Then set dead time to zero. The ceiling leaves the lower chart entirely and the best gain climbs to 4.97, but the match only reaches 79 per cent, because a proportional controller still settles short and no gain on the slider closes that gap. Tick show the same gain with no delay at any setting to see how much of the trouble on screen is the delay and how much was always going to be there.
