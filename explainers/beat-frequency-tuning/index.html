Set the coarse slider to eleven cents and look at the middle trace. The combined sound does not hold steady; it swells and dies away, over and over, a little under three times a second. Nothing about either string is changing. The pulsing is what two steady tones do when they are close but not equal, and it is the single most useful thing a tuner has, because the rate of it depends on the gap between the two pitches and on nothing else at all.

Why two steady tones pulse

Add two cosines of equal strength and a schoolbook identity rearranges them into a product:

cos(2πf₁t) + cos(2πf₂t) = 2 · cos(πΔf·t) · cos(2πf̅·t) Δf = f₁ − f₂ f̅ = (f₁ + f₂) / 2

The right-hand side is one fast wave at the average of the two pitches, multiplied by one slow wave at half their difference. Your ear cannot hear a wave at half a hertz as a pitch, so it hears the slow factor as loudness instead: a single tone, at the average pitch, getting louder and softer. That is the bottom trace. Drag the listening point across a swell and watch the two waves march into step; drag it into a dip and they are pointing opposite ways, cancelling each other on the way past.

The factor of two that catches people

The slow factor runs at Δf/2 cycles a second — half the difference, exactly as the identity says. But loudness does not care about sign. A trough where the slow factor is −1 sounds exactly as loud as a peak where it is +1, so the ear counts twice as many swells as the factor has cycles, and the beat rate comes out at the full Δf. Two strings a hertz apart beat once a second. The readout above tracks both numbers separately so you can watch the doubling happen.

Unequal strings never go quiet

Pull the loudness slider down and the dips stop reaching the floor. With different amplitudes the envelope runs between a₁ + a₂ at its loudest and the absolute difference at its quietest, so only a perfectly matched pair falls all the way to silence. The rate is untouched — still exactly Δf — which is why this works on real instruments, where no two strings ever speak at the same volume. It is the depth of the wobble, not its speed, that tells you the strings are matched in loudness.

Why tuners count beats instead of pitches

Hearing that one note is fifteen cents sharp is a trained skill. Counting three pulses a second is not. And the count is far more precise than the ear is: at A4, one single cent of error is a quarter of a hertz, a beat every four seconds, plainly visible. That precision is also how the awkward intervals get set. An equal-tempered fifth above A4 sits at 659.26 Hz while the pure fifth is 660, so a tuner setting that fifth listens for about three beats in four seconds and stops there deliberately, leaving it slightly impure on purpose.

What to try

Start at eleven cents and widen the window to six seconds to count the swells against the ticks on the baseline. Walk the coarse slider down toward zero and watch the swells stretch out until the trace goes flat. Then halve the remaining error with the fine slider and notice the beat rate halving with it. Finally turn on the mystery string, tune it by the meter alone until the wobble stops, and reveal how close you got.
