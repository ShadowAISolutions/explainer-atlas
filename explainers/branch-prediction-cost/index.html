The loop above adds up the numbers past a threshold. Both ribbons hold exactly the same numbers; the only difference is the order they arrive in. Red marks the branches the processor guessed wrong and had to throw away work for. At the default settings the sorted pass costs 8,220 cycles and the shuffled pass 22,010, for precisely the same 1010 additions.

A branch is a bet the machine places early

A modern core has a hundred or more instructions in flight and cannot afford to wait and see which way an if goes before fetching what follows it. So it guesses, runs ahead speculatively, and when the guess turns out wrong it discards everything downstream and restarts. The discarded work is the penalty. Fourteen cycles, the default here, is in the right neighbourhood for a deep out-of-order core.

cycles = 4 * iterations + penalty * mispredictions

Sorting removes no additions whatsoever. The two arrays are permutations of one another, and the page checks that the branch is taken an identical number of times in both. What sorting changes is whether the branch's answer can be anticipated: the sorted sequence is a run of not-taken followed by a run of taken, with exactly one transition in it, and a two-bit counter mispredicts at most twice across the entire array. Shuffled, the same branch is a coin flip and it gets more than 46 per cent of them wrong.

The two-bit saturating counter is usually introduced as the strictly better version of "guess whatever happened last time", on the grounds that it demands two wrong guesses in a row before changing its mind. On random data that is true: at p = 0.7 it reaches 63.8 per cent against the one-bit predictor's 58.0. On repeating patterns it is frequently false. Run every non-constant repeating pattern of length two to twelve — 8166 of them — and the one-bit predictor is strictly more accurate on 3515, a little over two fifths of them. The cleanest case is NNTT, where one bit gets half right and the counter gets a quarter: the hysteresis is exactly as long as the runs, so the counter spends its entire life halfway between two minds and arrives at each turn facing the wrong way.

Random data has a ceiling, and the counter sits under it

Feed the counter independent coin flips that come up taken with probability p. The four states form a birth-and-death chain whose stationary weights are powers of p over one minus p, and the accuracy collapses to a closed form.

accuracy = (p^3 + q^3) / (p^2 + q^2)

At p = 0.7 that is 0.638. The best any fixed rule can manage is to name the commoner outcome every time, which scores 0.700. The counter falls six points short, and it falls short at every p strictly between zero and one, because a chain that keeps moving keeps spending time in states that disagree with the majority. It is an easy thing to state wrongly: the counter does not converge to the larger of p and one minus p. It converges to something strictly below it.

What history buys

Real predictors are not bare counters, because a bare counter has nowhere to put the information that actually matters. Index the counters by the last few outcomes instead of keeping a single one, and periodic patterns become learnable. One bit of history takes strict alternation from at best a coin to perfect, because the previous outcome alone names the next. NNTT needs two bits, and with two it is exact. With eight bits of history all 256 patterns of period eight are learned perfectly: a window as long as the period always identifies the phase.

What to try

Start from v >= 128 and drag the threshold. At either end the branch goes one way every time and both orders are free. In between, the sorted pass stays nearly free while the shuffled one climbs to its worst at the middle. Then type i % 4 < 2: sorting now does nothing at all, because the condition never looks at the values — and switch the predictor to two bits of history to watch the same pattern go to a hundred per cent.
