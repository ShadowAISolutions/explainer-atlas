Every Group One Way, Everyone the Other

Group B is admitted at a higher rate in the selective programme, and at a higher rate in the open one. Pooled, Group B is admitted at a lower rate than Group A. Nothing has been faked. Both statements are arithmetic, and both are true.

Rates do not care. Counts are conserved: every applicant lands in exactly one row.

Two programmes admit applicants. The selective one takes one in five; the open one takes three in four. Group B is a little stronger, so in each programme it is admitted a few points more often than Group A. Read the top band: two rows, and in both of them the red bar is longer than the blue one. Then read the bottom band, where the same applicants have simply been added together, and the blue bar is longer by twenty-four points.

No row was altered between the two bands. The only thing that changed is which rows the applicants were counted in.

A rate is an average with weights

A pooled rate is not the average of the row rates. It is the average weighted by where that group's applicants actually are:

pooled = SUM w(row) * rate(row) over rows that group's applicants in the row w(row) = ---------------------- that group's applicants in total

Each group brings its own weights. Group A puts a quarter of itself into the selective programme; Group B puts four fifths. So even with identical row rates the two pooled numbers would differ, because they are averages of the same list taken with different weights. Group B is mostly being averaged over the hard row.

Splitting the gap in two

The pooled gap comes apart exactly, with no remainder, into the part that is about treatment and the part that is about placement:

within = SUM wB * [ rateB - rateA ] mix = SUM [ wB - wA ] * rateA gap = within + mix (exactly, no remainder) all sums run over the rows, and w is that group's share of its own applicants in the row

The first sum asks what happens if you keep Group B where it is and give it Group A's rates: it is the advantage, averaged over B's own rows. The second asks what happens if you keep Group A's rates and only move people around. At the default settings the first sum is worth about six points in B's favour and the second about thirty against, and thirty beats six. That is the whole paradox, and the page shows both numbers under the chart.

It also tells you when a reversal is impossible. If both groups spread themselves across the rows in the same proportions, the two weightings are identical, the mix term is zero, and the pooled gap is a weighted average of the row gaps. An average cannot be outside the range of what it averages, so if every row favours B, the pooled figure must too. Drag the two mix sliders together and watch the flag disappear. Choose the matched-mix case and no setting of the other sliders can bring it back.

Berkeley, and a warning about the story

The famous case is the 1973 graduate admissions at Berkeley. Men were admitted at 44.5 per cent and women at 30.4, a fourteen-point gap that looks damning until you split by department, where women were admitted at the higher rate in four of the six largest. Splitting the gap gives a within term of one tenth of a point and a mix term of fourteen. Essentially the entire difference is placement: women applied in far greater numbers to the departments that admitted almost nobody.

Note what the decomposition does not say. It does not say the mix is innocent. Something put those applicants in those departments, and the arithmetic is silent on what. Simpson's paradox is not a debunking device; it is a warning that a pooled number answers a different question from the one you probably asked.

What to try

Set both mix sliders to the same value and sweep the rate sliders through their whole range: the flag never lights. Then separate the mixes by ten points and find the smallest Group B advantage that still reverses. Switch to the kidney stone trial, where open surgery beat the keyhole procedure on small stones and on large stones and lost overall, because the surgeons sent the hard cases to surgery. Then ask which number you would want if the stone were yours.
