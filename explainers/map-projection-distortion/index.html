Every Flat Map Is Wrong, and You Pick How

The lattice is what turns a single measurement into a picture of the whole map.

Put a small circle on a globe and project it. What lands on the paper is, to first order, an ellipse. Its longer semi-axis a and shorter one b are the two principal scale factors, and between them they say everything about what the map did to that spot. If a equals b the circle stays a circle and angles survive. If the product ab equals one the ellipse has the area the circle had. If both hold the map is perfect there, and a perfect map is exactly what you cannot have.

Why not

A sphere of radius one has Gaussian curvature one at every point. A sheet of paper has zero. Gauss's Theorema Egregium says curvature survives any bending that does not stretch, so a map that preserved every distance would have to carry curvature one onto a surface with curvature zero. There is no such map, and no amount of cleverness produces one. Every projection is a choice about where to put the damage.

Two choices are so useful they have names. A conformal map sets a = b everywhere, so shapes are right in the small and the area scale is forced to be a². An equal-area map sets ab = 1 everywhere, so the ellipse always has the right area and therefore must be the wrong shape wherever a is not 1. You cannot have both without having neither problem, which is to say without an isometry, which does not exist.

Reading the numbers

area scale = a * b angle error = 2 * asin( (a-b) / (a+b) ) Mercator a = b = sec(lat) so area = sec(lat)^2 equal-area a * b = 1 exactly

Mercator's area scale is the square of the secant, which is why it is the projection people complain about. At 60 degrees every length doubles and every area is four times too big; at 75 degrees areas are inflated about fifteenfold. Nothing about this is a mistake. Mercator was drawn so that a constant compass bearing is a straight line on the chart, and buying that costs exactly this.

Set the projection to Lambert and the rings snap to a constant area and turn into flattened lozenges near the poles: a = sec(lat), b = cos(lat), product one. That is the same secant, moved out of the area column and into the shape column.

Where a map is honest

Move the standard parallel and watch the undistorted band move with it. Both cylindricals are exact along their standard parallel and nowhere else. The sinusoidal is different: it is exact along the whole central meridian and along the equator, and shears badly at the far corners, which is why atlases cut it into lobes. Mollweide is equal-area everywhere and exact nowhere at all — it spends its whole budget on keeping area right and looking like a globe.

What to try

Put the circle on Reykjavik in Mercator and read the area scale, then switch to Mollweide without moving it. Then take Mercator's standard question: drag along the equator and watch every number sit still, because a cylindrical projection's distortion depends only on latitude. Finally set the sinusoidal and drag along the central meridian, where a and b are both exactly one from pole to pole, then step sideways and watch the ring shear.
