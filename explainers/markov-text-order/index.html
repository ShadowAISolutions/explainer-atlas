Order Two Is Babble, Order Four Is Plagiarism

A Markov model remembers the last few characters and nothing else. Give it one character of memory and it babbles; give it four and it starts quoting its source back at you word for word. The useful band in between is narrower than anyone expects.

The model in the panel above is nothing but a table of counts. For every stretch of text of a given length, it records which characters were seen following it and how often. To write, it looks at what it has just produced, finds that stretch in the table, and rolls a die weighted by those counts. That is the entire algorithm; there is no grammar in it and no idea of a word.

At a memory of one character the output is obvious nonsense, but it is not random nonsense. Vowels and consonants alternate about as often as they do in English, the letter e turns up more than the letter z, and spaces arrive at roughly the right rate. The model has learned the statistics of single characters and nothing else, and that is already enough to make it look like language from a distance.

The cliff

Push the memory to three and words appear — and the shading in the upper panel appears with them. Those are the stretches of twelve characters or more that the model did not invent but copied. By four almost nothing is left that is not copied. The lower panel plots that share against memory, and the shape of it is the point of the page: on this eight-hundred-character passage the model is original at two, more than half copied at three, and reciting at four.

copied share at memory 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 → 0, 0, 57, 95, 100, 100 per cent

The reason is counting, not cleverness. A training text of eight hundred characters contains about eight hundred stretches of length four, and most of them occur exactly once. A context that occurred once has exactly one recorded continuation, so the die has one face. The model is not choosing at that point; it is reciting. Watch the choices-per-context readout fall toward one as you raise the memory, and the copied share rise to meet it. A longer source pushes the whole curve to the right — that is the only thing that moves it — which is why a model with a useful amount of memory needs an enormous amount of text.

Why more memory always looks better

The bits-per-character line is how surprised the model is by the very text it was trained on, and it falls with every extra character of memory, all the way to zero. That is not evidence the model is learning language. Measured on its own training text, more memory can never look worse, because the model is allowed to memorise the answer. A score that a model can improve by memorising is not a measure of understanding, which is why any honest evaluation holds text back and scores the model on words it has never seen.

What to try

Choose the nursery rhyme and set the memory to two. The rhyme repeats itself so heavily that two characters are already enough to pin down where you are in it, and two-fifths of the output is shaded while the same setting on the Austen passage copies nothing at all. Then paste in a paragraph of your own and find the memory where it stops babbling: that number is a property of your text, not of the model. Short and repetitive texts break early, long and varied ones hold out longer.
