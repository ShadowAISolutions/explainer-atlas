Even Noise Slopes Down

Word frequency really does fall off as roughly one over rank. But sorting counts by size and drawing the line is a procedure that slopes downward whether there is a law underneath it or not — and on a short text it slopes just as steeply on pure noise.

The passage loaded above is seventeen hundred words about a harbour town. The word the appears in it two hundred and nine times. The second most common word appears a hundred times, the third forty-four, the tenth twenty-five. Rank the words by how often they turn up and the counts fall away in a shape so regular that you can almost read the next one off the last. More than half of the different words in the passage appear exactly once, and never again.

That shape is Zipf's law, and it holds in every language anyone has checked, in speech and in writing, in the nineteenth century and in this one. Plot rank against frequency with both axes on log scales and the law becomes a straight line.

f(r) is proportional to r to the power minus s, so log f(r) = constant minus s times log r

Fit a line to those points and the slope is minus s. For large corpora s comes out close to one, which is where the usual way of putting it comes from: the second word is about half as common as the first, the tenth about a tenth as common, and so on down.

The procedure is not innocent

Now choose a source with no law at all. Those words are drawn uniformly: every word is exactly as likely as every other, and there is no law to find because none was put there. Count them, sort them, plot them, fit the line. The slope is not zero. Drawn to the harbour passage's own length, with as many different words in it, it comes out near minus a sixth, and that is not the luck of one draw — repeat it two dozen times and not one of them comes out flat.

The reason is that ranking is not a neutral act. Rank one is not a word, it is whichever word came out on top, and with four hundred and fifty words competing for seventeen hundred draws the winner is the luckiest of four hundred and fifty. Rank four hundred is the unluckiest. Sorting turns the spread of random counts into a curve, and the curve always runs downhill, because that is what sorting means.

Push words counted down to a hundred and the trap closes. The harbour passage over its first hundred words fits a slope of minus 0.510. A hundred words of pure noise, with as many different words in them, fits minus 0.437. Those are not the same number, but they are seven hundredths apart, where at full length the two are separated by two thirds — nine times the gap. At a hundred words almost the entire slope is the sorting, and a paragraph of your own writing, dropped into the box above, is exactly that length.

What the evidence actually is

The lower chart is the honest version. It draws the fitted slope against how many words were counted, for the chosen text and, as the ochre band, for the same number of words with no law in them. On the left the two overlap. As the count grows the band climbs back toward zero, because more draws per word means less of the ordering is luck, while the real text stays down. By seventeen hundred words the passage sits at minus 0.831 and the noise at minus 0.163. Going the other way the band stops moving: by about two hundred words nearly every word in the passage has been used once, so there is hardly anything left for the sort to order and the phantom has gone as far as it can.

That gap is the finding. The slope on its own is not: it drifts with the length of the text, with how many ranks you fit over, and with nothing at all. Drag ranks fitted across its range on the full passage and the answer moves by about a tenth without a single word changing.

The two ends of the line

The points are not straight everywhere, and both departures mean something. At the very top a handful of function words sit below the line: grammar caps how often even the can be used. At the bottom the points stop dead at a frequency of one, because a word cannot appear half a time, and that floor is where most of the vocabulary lives — every word used once, in a row, at ranks two hundred and upward. Count more text and that floor moves right rather than down: new words keep arriving, more slowly than the words themselves, which is the companion law about how a vocabulary grows.

What to try

Set words counted to a hundred and step through all four sources. Every one of them slopes down and three of the four give about the same answer. Then walk the count back up to seventeen hundred and watch which one stays. Finally put your own writing in the box, press count my text, and read the noise figure beside the fitted slope before believing the fitted slope.
