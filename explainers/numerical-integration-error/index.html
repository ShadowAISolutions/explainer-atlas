Why Simpson's Rule Beats the Trapezoid

Both rules chop a curve into strips and add up the pieces. Halve the strip width and one rule's error falls by a factor of four; the other's falls by sixteen. The extra cost is a single multiplication.

Every numerical integration rule is the same bet: replace the function you have with a function you can integrate exactly, then integrate that instead. The trapezoid rule bets on straight lines. Over each strip it draws a chord from one endpoint to the other and takes the area underneath. Simpson's rule bets on parabolas, fitting one through three consecutive points and integrating that.

The trapezoid rule is the obvious idea, and it works. Push the strip count up and the estimate converges. But look at the lower panel with sin x selected: double the strips from 8 to 16 and the red error drops by a factor of four, not two. Double again and it drops by four again. That is second-order convergence — error proportional to h², where h is the strip width.

Where the four comes from

Expand the function about the midpoint of a strip and integrate term by term. The constant and linear parts are captured exactly by the chord — a straight line through two points on a straight line is that line. The first term the chord gets wrong is the curvature, and the error it leaves on a single strip works out to

E_strip = −(h³/12) · f″(ξ)

for some point ξ inside the strip. There are (b − a)/h strips, so the h³ per strip becomes h² overall. That is the whole story of the trapezoid rule: it is exact for anything a straight line can represent, and it pays h² for everything else.

Buying two more orders for free

Simpson's rule fits a parabola through three points, so you would expect it to be exact for quadratics and to leave an h³ error. It is exact for quadratics — and also, surprisingly, for cubics. Select x³ − x and set the strips to 2: the blue error is zero, to the last bit of double precision, using a single parabola to integrate a cubic.

The reason is symmetry. A parabola fitted through three equally spaced points differs from a cubic by a term proportional to (x − x₀)(x − x₁)(x − x₂), and that product is odd about the middle point. Integrating an odd function over a symmetric interval gives exactly zero. The cubic error term does not shrink — it cancels. So the first surviving term is the fourth derivative, and the error per pair of strips is

E_pair = −(h⁵/90) · f⁗(ξ)

which accumulates to h⁴ overall. Halving h divides the error by sixteen. In exchange, Simpson's rule weights the interior points 4, 2, 4, 2 instead of all 1. It evaluates the function at exactly the same points as the trapezoid rule. The extra accuracy costs nothing but the weights.

What to try

Switch to √x. The neat slopes collapse: Simpson's line flattens towards the trapezoid's. The derivation above quietly assumed f⁗ exists and is bounded, and √x has an infinite first derivative at the origin. No amount of cleverness in the weights survives a function that is not smooth where you are sampling it.

Then pull the error floor down to 1e-14 with sin x and push the strips up. Simpson's line stops falling and starts wandering. That is not the method failing — it is the method succeeding past the point where double precision can represent the answer. Below about 1e-15 relative error you are measuring rounding, not truncation.
