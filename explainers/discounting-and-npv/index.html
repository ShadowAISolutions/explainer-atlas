A sea wall costs a thousand now and pays back slowly, twenty in the first year, forty in the second, rising by twenty a year for twenty years. A retrofit costs the same thousand and pays a flat hundred and fifty every year for the same twenty. Over the whole period the sea wall pays out four thousand two hundred and the retrofit three thousand. The sea wall is obviously better, and for most of the plausible range of discount rates it is obviously worse.

What a discount rate does

Discounting says a payment of C arriving in t years is worth less today, by a factor that compounds:

NPV = Σ Ct / (1 + r)t

At six per cent, a hundred pounds in thirty years is worth 17.41 today. At ten per cent it is 5.73. Nothing about the payment changed; the rate did all of it. That is why the sea wall, whose money is mostly late, loses so much more than the retrofit when the rate rises. Drag the rate and watch the two curves converge, touch at 10.031780 per cent, and change places.

The retrofit is a level annuity, so it has a closed form rather than a sum: 150 × (1 − (1+r)−n) / r, less the thousand up front. The page checks that against a term-by-term sum at a hundred combinations of rate and horizon and they agree to about 10−12.

Where the rate of return goes wrong

The internal rate of return is the discount rate at which a project is worth exactly nothing. It sounds like a property of the project, and it usually behaves like one, which is why it is quoted so often. It is really just a root of a polynomial, and polynomials do not promise one root.

Choose a mine that must be cleaned up. A thousand to open it, twenty-five hundred back in the first year, and then fifteen hundred and sixty owed at the end for making the site safe. That flow is worth nothing at twenty per cent and worth nothing again at thirty, both exactly. Between those rates it is worth something positive; outside them it is worth less than nothing. Asking for "the" return is asking the wrong question.

The second curve is the same mine with a cleanup bill of sixteen hundred instead. It has exactly the same two changes of sign, and it has no internal rate of return at all: at every discount rate from zero upward it is worth less than nothing, and the best it ever manages is minus 23.4375 at a rate of exactly twenty-eight per cent. Two sign changes permit two roots; they do not supply them.

For ever is nearer than it looks

The last pair puts a forty-year annuity beside a payment that never stops. The perpetuity is worth C/r, and the annuity is short of it by exactly C(1+r)−n/r, which the page checks at forty rates. At eight per cent a forty-year run has already captured ninety-five per cent of an infinite one. Push the horizon far enough and the annuity formula does not merely approach the perpetuity, it becomes it: the discount factor underflows and the two expressions are the same number, bit for bit.

What to try
