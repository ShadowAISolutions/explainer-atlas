The Rule of 72 Is Only Honest at One Rate

Money that earns interest on its interest doubles on a fixed clock, and that clock depends on nothing but the rate. The famous shortcut for reading it off is exactly right at one rate and drifts either side of it.

One pound, left alone at seven per cent. After a year it is 1.07. After two it is not 1.14 but 1.1449, because the seven pence earned its own interest. That second-order penny is the whole subject. It is also why the balance has no natural scale: at every point on the curve the account is growing by the same fraction of itself, so the time it takes to double is the same wherever you start measuring from.

Scrub the year marker and watch the horizontal rules go by. They sit at two, four, eight and sixteen times the opening deposit, and the marker crosses them at evenly spaced years. That even spacing is the definition of exponential growth, and it is what makes a single number — the doubling time — enough to describe the whole account.

The clock behind the curve

balance = (1 + r)t, doubling time = ln 2 / ln(1 + r)

Set the balance to two and solve for the year. Nothing survives but the rate. A hundred pounds and a hundred thousand pounds double on exactly the same schedule, which is the fact that makes compound growth so hard to feel: the early years look like nothing happening and the late years look like a different process entirely, when it is the same constant clock throughout.

Where seventy-two comes from

For small rates, ln(1 + r) is nearly r, so the doubling time is nearly ln 2 divided by r, which is 0.693 over r — the rule of 70. Seventy-two is not the better approximation. It is the more divisible number, and it happens to be the one that corrects for the fact that ln(1 + r) is a little smaller than r, which makes the true doubling time a little longer than 0.693 / r.

The two errors cancel exactly once. Drag the rate and watch the lower chart: the rule of 72 crosses zero error at 7.85 per cent and is wrong either side of it. It stays within half a per cent only between about 6.8 and 8.9 per cent, and within two per cent across the familiar 4 to 12 band. Push the rate past 19.2 per cent and the shortcut is out by more than five per cent; by thirty per cent it claims 2.40 years against a true 2.64, an error of nine per cent and always in the optimistic direction.

Adding the interest more often

Change how often the interest is credited and the curve lifts, because interest added in June spends the second half of the year earning interest of its own. The lift shrinks as the periods get shorter — halving the period halves what is left to gain — and it converges on a ceiling rather than running away. That ceiling is continuous compounding, where the balance is ert and the doubling time collapses to a clean ln 2 over r, with no correction term at all. The rule of 70 is exactly right there, and the rule of 72 is wrong everywhere.

What to try

Park the rate at 7.85 per cent and watch the error read zero. Walk it down to two per cent and the shortcut becomes over-cautious by several per cent; walk it up to thirty and it flatters the account badly. Then set the rate to ten and step the compounding from yearly to continuous: the effective annual rate climbs from 10.00 to 10.52 per cent and stops, and the doubling time falls from 7.27 years to 6.93 and stops with it. The gap between those two numbers is the entire value of compounding more often, and it is smaller than most people expect.
