Where The Ideal Gas Law Quietly Stops Working

An ideal gas is a gas whose molecules have no size and no interest in each other. Neither is true, and the surprise is how little it matters. At room temperature and ordinary pressure, carbon dioxide obeys PV = nRT to within a per cent or so. Squeeze it into a tenth of the volume and the error is no longer small.

ideal: P = nRT/V     van der Waals: P = nRT/(V − nb) − an²/V²

Van der Waals patched the two false assumptions one at a time. Molecules occupy space, so the volume they can move in is not V but V−nb; the correction makes the pressure higher. Molecules attract one another, so a molecule about to strike the wall is tugged back by the ones behind it; that term is proportional to the square of the density and makes the pressure lower. The two corrections fight, and which one wins is the whole shape of the curve.

Reading the shaded area

At large volume both terms vanish and the curve lies on the line. Squeeze, and the attraction term grows as 1/V² while the repulsion term only matters once V approaches nb, so attraction gets there first: the real pressure falls below ideal and the compressibility factor Z = PV/nRT drops under one. Squeeze further and the excluded volume takes over, Z climbs back through one and keeps going. The strip under the axis is that story in one dimension, and the crossing point is where the two corrections exactly cancel.

The critical point falls out of the algebra

Cool the gas and the curve develops a wiggle — a stretch where squeezing the gas lowers its pressure, which nothing stable can do. That is the model telling you the gas is condensing. The temperature at which the wiggle first appears is the critical temperature, and it can be read straight off the constants: Tc = 8a/27Rb, with Pc = a/27b² and Vc = 3nb. For carbon dioxide those give 304.0 K and 74.0 bar, against measured values of 304.1 and 73.8. The page also finds the same point numerically, by bisecting on the temperature at which the wiggle disappears, and asserts the two routes agree.

The combination PcVc/RTc comes out as exactly three eighths for every gas, which is the model's most obvious failure: real gases cluster around 0.27. Van der Waals gets the shape right and the details wrong, which is roughly what you should expect from two correction terms and no adjustable curve-fitting.

What to try

Leave carbon dioxide at 300 K and press halve the volume four times in a row. The first press moves the pressure by slightly less than the ideal law predicts; by the fourth it moves by more, and the strip beneath the axis has changed colour under your marker. Then set the temperature to 250 K, below the critical 304 K, and look for the wiggle at the left-hand end. Helium at the same settings will not show one until you get below about five kelvin.
