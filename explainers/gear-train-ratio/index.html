The Gear in the Middle Does Nothing

Drop a wheel between two gears and the output turns the other way at exactly the same speed as before. Its tooth count cancels itself out. Put two wheels on one shaft instead and nothing cancels, which is how a clock gets 12 to 1 out of parts that fit in a pocket.

    A mesh is a bargain between two tooth counts. Where two wheels touch, their rims travel together, so a wheel with half the teeth has to turn twice as fast. Everything a gear train does follows from applying that one sentence along a line of wheels.

    Why the middle wheel cancels

    Follow three wheels in a row, each meshing the next. The first mesh multiplies the speed by N1/N2, the second by N2/N3. Multiply them:

    out N1 N2 N1 --- = -- * -- = -- in N2 N3 N3

    The middle count appears once above and once below and leaves. Add ten more wheels between and the same thing happens ten more times: every intermediate tooth count cancels against itself, and a simple train's ratio is the first wheel over the last, no matter what is in between. Set the train to a single pair, read the ratio, then raise the wheel count to three and give the new middle wheel any tooth count you like. The ratio does not move.

    What does move is the direction. Meshed wheels turn opposite ways, so each mesh flips the sign and the output direction is minus one raised to the number of meshes. That is what an idler is actually for: it is a reversing gear, and it is also how a designer bridges a gap between two shafts that have to stay where they are.

    Two wheels on one shaft

    The cancellation had a hidden condition: each intermediate wheel had to be both the driven wheel of one mesh and the driver of the next, so its count appeared in both positions. Bolt a second wheel to the same shaft and that stops being true. The pair shares a speed but not a tooth count, so the ratio you divide by is not the ratio you then multiply by, and the stages stay multiplied:

    out N1 N3 --- = -- * -- in N2 N4 N2 and N3 share a shaft

    Two modest stages of one to four and one to three give twelve to one in a space a single pair could not manage without a wheel twelve times the size of its partner. The clockwork preset is exactly that, and the fraction readout shows it reduced by exact integer arithmetic rather than by rounding.

    What you pay

    Ideal gears carry power straight through, so torque goes up by exactly the factor speed goes down. A train that slows the output twelvefold multiplies torque twelvefold, and the readouts show the product holding constant to the last digit as you drag anything.

    What to try

    Start from the single pair and note the ratio and the direction. Add one wheel and watch the direction flip while the ratio sits still; sweep that wheel's teeth from 8 to 60 to confirm it truly does not matter. Then select the third wheel, tick it onto the previous shaft, and watch the same three wheels produce a completely different number.
