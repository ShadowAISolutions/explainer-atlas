Set the tolerance to 37.5 per cent and press Run to a standstill. Every square on the board has just declared that it is happy to be a minority — three like neighbours out of eight is enough — and the board still lands somewhere near three-quarters same-colour, in solid patches with clean edges. Nobody on this board wants segregation. They got it anyway.

What each square is doing

A square looks at its eight neighbours, ignores the empty ones, and works out what fraction of the rest share its colour. If that fraction is below the threshold it moves to an empty cell; otherwise it stays. That is the whole rule. There is no preference for a block, no aversion to the other colour, no coordination, and no planner.

The board is scored by the simplest measure available: take every pair of neighbouring occupied cells and ask what share of those pairs are the same colour.

segregation = same-colour adjacent pairs ------------------------- all adjacent occupied pairs side n = 20, no empty cells: checkerboard (n-1)/(2n-1) -> 0.487 stripes n/(2(2n-1)) -> 0.256 two blocks 1 - (3n-2)/E -> 0.961 E = 2(n-1)(2n-1) adjacent pairs

A random mix sits at about 0.50 because a coin flip makes half the pairs match. Load each of those three starting boards in turn and the readout will show you those exact numbers before you press anything — the page checks its own scoring against all three closed forms, and computes the score a second time by a different route, pair by pair rather than square by square, and demands the two agree.

Why the drift only goes one way

Dissatisfaction is local, but a move is not. When a square leaves, it takes its colour out of one neighbourhood and puts it into another, which makes some of its new neighbours more content and some of its old ones less. Contentment does not rise steadily — push the tolerance high and the count of discontented squares bounces around for a while. What does rise is the score, because every move that fixes a square's own neighbourhood does so by putting like next to like, and there is no move in the opposite direction. The asymmetry is the whole mechanism.

Push the threshold up in steps and watch where the curve bends. Below about a quarter almost nothing happens. Between a quarter and a half the board goes from barely sorted to strongly sorted. Above a half everyone is demanding a majority, and the outcome is near-total separation — but that is the boring case, because there the outcome does match the preference.

What to try

Load the perfect checkerboard, set the tolerance to 37.5 per cent, and run it. Every square starts in a four-out-of-eight tie and the board is the most integrated arrangement that exists; it still falls apart. Then load already two blocks and run it with the tolerance at zero: nothing moves, because nobody is discontented, and segregation is perfectly stable once reached even though nobody is enforcing it.

Then paint your own. Choose the blank board, draw whatever arrangement you think should survive, and press run. The point of the page is not that the model predicts any real neighbourhood — it does not. The point is that a photograph of the finished board tells you almost nothing about what the squares wanted.
