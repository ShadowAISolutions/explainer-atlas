Start with the harmonics slider at 1. That is a pure sine wave, and it is the only sound in music that has no timbre to speak of — a flute played softly is about as close as an instrument gets. Now drag the slider right. Nothing is being filtered, distorted or shaped. Sine waves at 2, 3, 4 times the fundamental are simply being added, at amplitudes taken from the recipe, and a square wave appears.

The only number that matters is the ratio

A periodic sound of frequency f₀ can contain energy at f₀, 2f₀, 3f₀ and so on, and at nothing in between — anything else would not repeat at the right rate. Fourier's theorem says that is not merely allowed but complete: every reasonable waveform of period T is a sum of sines at whole multiples of 1/T, and the amplitudes are determined uniquely by

aₙ = (2/T) ∫ x(t) · sin(2πnt/T) dt over one period

So a timbre is a list. Push the fundamental slider from 220 Hz to 440 and every partial doubles with it; the shape on screen does not change at all, because the shape depends only on the ratios between the amplitudes, never on the pitch. This is why you recognise a clarinet across its whole range.

Three recipes, three shapes

The square wave uses odd harmonics only, falling as 1/n: 4/π times 1, a third, a fifth, a seventh. Drop the even harmonics from any sound and you get that hollow, woody quality — a clarinet's lowest register does roughly this, because a tube closed at one end supports only odd modes. The sawtooth keeps every harmonic at 1/n and sounds brighter and brassier; bowed strings are close to it. The triangle keeps odd harmonics but falls as 1/n², which is very much faster, so it is nearly a sine with a hint of edge.

That last difference is worth watching. Set the recipe to square with 32 harmonics: the readout says 11.3 per cent of the waveform's amplitude is still missing, and quadrupling the partials only halves it. Switch to triangle and the same 32 partials leave 0.22 per cent behind — fifty times less — because there, quadrupling the partials divides the shortfall by eight. Smooth waveforms need few harmonics; waveforms with a jump in them need infinitely many and never quite get there.

The corner that will not lie flat

The stubborn overshoot at the square wave's vertical edge is the Gibbs phenomenon, and it is one of the genuinely surprising results in analysis. As you add harmonics the ripple gets narrower and moves closer to the edge, but its height does not shrink. It converges on a fixed 8.95 per cent of the size of the jump, forever, for any function with a discontinuity. Where the square wave is exactly 1, the partial sum peaks at 1.17898 whether you use nine harmonics or nine hundred, and that number is (2/π) times the integral of sin t over t from 0 to π — an integral with no Fourier series anywhere in it.

What does shrink is the area under the ripple, which is why the series still converges in the sense that matters for energy. Parseval's identity says the mean square of the sum is exactly half the sum of the squared amplitudes:

RMS² = ½ · (a₁² + a₂² + a₃² + …)

For the square wave those squares are 1, 1/9, 1/25, 1/49, and they add to π²/8, which is exactly what makes the root mean square come out at 1. Every harmonic you leave off is a specific, countable amount of missing energy.

What to try

Put the recipe on sawtooth and step the harmonic count from 1 to 6 one click at a time. The ramp is recognisable by about 4, and the last 26 partials are only sharpening the corner. Then switch to soft and notice that the second harmonic is already down at a quarter and the shape barely moves after 5 — a spectrum that falls as 1/n² is nearly finished before it starts.

Finally, press Listen on square and then on soft at the same pitch and the same harmonic count. Same note, same fundamental, same everything a tuner would measure. They do not sound remotely alike, and the only difference between them is the list.
