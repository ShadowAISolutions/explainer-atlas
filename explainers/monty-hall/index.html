The objection everyone raises is the same, and it sounds airtight: two doors are left, one has the car, so it is fifty-fifty. The trouble is that the two remaining doors did not get there the same way.

Follow the information

Your door was chosen before anything was known. It had a one-in-three chance then and nothing that happened afterwards touched it — the host was never going to open your door, so watching him not open it tells you nothing about it. That door is frozen at 1/3.

The other two doors started with 2/3 between them. The host then removed one of them, and here is the thing: he removed it on purpose. He looked, and he opened a door he knew was empty. The 2/3 did not evaporate with the door he opened; it could not, because he was never going to open the car. All of it lands on the single door he left standing.

P(car behind your door) = 1/3 P(car behind the other two) = 2/3 → all of it on the one still closed

So switching is a two-thirds bet against a one-third bet. The fifty-fifty intuition treats the host as a coin. He is not; he is a filter with information you do not have.

Take the knowledge away

Switch the host to knows nothing. Now he opens a random door, and sometimes he reveals the car — those rounds are void and get thrown out. Play the rest and the advantage is gone: switching and staying both win half the time.

Nothing about the doors changed. What changed is that an open goat door no longer carries information, because a goat is simply what you usually get when you open a door at random. The surviving rounds are exactly those where both remaining doors are equally likely, so they are. The entire 2/3 result lives in the host's knowledge, not in the geometry of the problem — which is why the puzzle is so much better as a lesson about conditioning than as a lesson about doors.

Push the door count up

Drag the doors to twelve. Your first pick is right 1/12 of the time, so 11/12 of the probability sits elsewhere. The host opens one goat, and now the remaining mass is spread over ten closed doors rather than concentrated on one:

P(switch wins) = (N − 1) / (N(N − 2))

which at N = 12 is about 0.092 against staying's 0.083 — better, but barely. The famous version of this generalisation has the host open every other goat, leaving one door; that one carries the whole 11/12 and switching becomes overwhelming. Opening a single door instead, as here, spreads the same evidence thinly. It is the amount of information the host gives away that matters, not the drama of the reveal.

What to try

Set three doors, a knowing host, and 20000 simulated rounds. The simulated bar lands on 0.667 within a whisker. Then flip the host to ignorant and watch both bars snap to 0.5 — the clearest demonstration that this was never a puzzle about doors.
