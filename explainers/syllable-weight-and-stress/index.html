A Latin speaker never had to memorise where the accent went. Given the word, the rule produces it, and the rule is short enough to say out loud: if the word has one or two syllables, stress the first; otherwise look at the second-from-last syllable, and if it is heavy stress that, and if it is light stress the one before it.

Heavy and light

A syllable is heavy if it has a long vowel, a diphthong, or a consonant closing it. It is light if it is a short vowel with nothing after it. That is the only property of a syllable the rule can see. Not which vowel, not which consonants, not what the word means — just whether the syllable is, in the old metaphor, long by nature or long by position.

So a·mī·cus has a long ī in its second syllable, which is therefore heavy, and the stress lands there: a MĪ cus. But fa·ci·lis has a short open ci, so the rule skips back one more and gives FA ci lis. Two words of the same length, one syllable different in weight, and the accent moves.

Three states, read backwards

Written as a machine rather than a sentence, the rule reads the word from the right and needs three states and no memory beyond which state it is in:

ultima → skip it
penult → heavy? stress here : step back
antepenult → stress here, whatever its weight

It never reaches a fourth state, which is why Latin stress never falls further back than the third syllable from the end. The page checks this exhaustively: over all 510 weight patterns from one to eight syllables, the accent lands on the antepenult or later every single time. The machine and a direct index calculation are written separately here and agree on all 8,190 patterns up to twelve syllables.

The collapse

A word of n syllables has 2n possible weight patterns. A twelve-syllable word has 4,096 of them. The rule maps every one of those onto one of exactly two answers: the penult or the antepenult. Everything else about the word — ten of its twelve syllables and all of their weights — makes no difference whatever.

That is what the strip along the bottom shows. One thin column per pattern, coloured by where the stress fell. However many syllables you add, there are never more than two colours, and for one- and two-syllable words there is only one.

Where the rules part company

Switch to penultimate always, the Polish pattern, and the two rules agree on every word with a heavy penult and disagree on every word with a light one. Not approximately: exactly. Of the 510 patterns up to eight syllables, 252 have a light penult, and those 252 are precisely the ones on which the two rules give different answers. A weight-sensitive rule is a quantity-insensitive rule plus one exception, and the exception has a perfectly crisp shape.

What to try

Start from facilis, whose penult is light, and press flip the last twice to see the stress sit still — the ultima is the one syllable whose weight the rule never asks about. Then build a word of six light syllables and make the fifth one heavy: the accent jumps one place to the right and stops there, no matter what you do to the other five.
