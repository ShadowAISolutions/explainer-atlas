Say heed, then hod, and hold each vowel. Nothing about your vocal folds changed: they are buzzing at the same pitch either way. What changed is which frequencies the air in your mouth amplifies on the way out. Two of those resonances are enough to tell almost every vowel of English apart from every other, and this page is a map of the two.

They are called formants, numbered from the bottom: F1 and F2. The chart on the right is drawn the way phoneticians draw it, which looks backwards until you know why — F2 decreasing to the right, F1 increasing downwards. Do that and the resulting picture happens to match the tongue: high front vowels at the top left, low back vowels at the bottom right. The chart is a coordinate system that was later noticed to be a diagram.

Two numbers, ten regions

The ten marked points are the measured averages from Peterson and Barney's 1952 study of American English, still the reference. Every point on the plane is shaded by which of the ten it is closest to, measured in log frequency, because hearing is roughly logarithmic. Drag the marker across a boundary and the name changes at exactly the line where the distances tie.

The boundaries are the point. Vowels are not ten discrete things with gaps between them: they are ten places to stand in a continuous space, and any point in it is pronounceable. Cross the boundary between hod and hawed slowly and you pass through everything in between, all of it a real sound that some accent somewhere uses.

Where the numbers come from

The tract is, acoustically, a tube about 17 cm long, closed at the vocal folds and open at the lips. An even tube of that length resonates at odd multiples of a quarter wavelength:

fₙ = (2n − 1) · c / 4L  →  500, 1500, 2500 Hz

which is the neutral vowel, the one in the middle of the chart. Put the tongue somewhere and you have split the tube in two: a back cavity and a front cavity meeting at a constriction. Set the area ratio to one and the two cavities become a single even tube again, whatever their lengths — the panel underneath shows it snapping back to 500 and 1500. Push the ratio away from one and the two cavities start resonating more on their own, F1 falling and F2 rising, until each is close to its own quarter wavelength.

What to try

Drag the marker to the top left, around F1 270 and F2 2290, and you have heed. Now drag straight down, keeping F2 where it is: the vowel goes to hid, then head, then had. That single downward line is your jaw opening.

Then work the tube. Leave the area ratio at one and change the two cavity lengths: the formants depend only on the total, not on where the split is. Now set the ratio to about five — a narrow constriction at the front — and watch the hollow marker swing towards the low-F1, high-F2 corner.

Finally press Child-sized tract. Every cavity shrinks by about a quarter and every formant rises by exactly the reciprocal of that: the resonances of a tube scale as 1/L, and nothing else about the vowel changes. A child saying hod and an adult saying hod are at different points on this chart, which is why the regions drawn here fit adult male voices and have to be redrawn for anyone else.
