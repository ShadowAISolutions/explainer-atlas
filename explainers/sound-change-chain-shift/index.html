Chaucer's name rhymed with the modern calm, his meet sounded like today's mate, and his house was closer to hoose. Two hundred years later every one of those long vowels had moved, and the spelling never caught up. That is why English writes the same letter for the vowel of bite and the vowel of machine, and why no one can learn to spell it.

The thing that did not happen

Seven classes of words moved. Push the stage slider and watch them: bite slides down and turns into a glide, meet climbs into the space it left, meat climbs into the space meet left, and so on down the front of the mouth. The back of the mouth does the same thing at the same time. What never happens is a collision. At no stage do two of the seven classes end up sounding alike.

That is remarkable, because a vowel has nowhere much to go. The mouth is small, the vowels are packed into it, and a change of a few per cent in tongue height is enough to turn one into another. If each vowel had drifted on its own, the seven would have been five within a century. Instead they moved as a system, and the bar chart under the vowel chart is the record of it: the closest any two classes come to each other, stage by stage.

Pull or push

Two accounts fit the endpoints and disagree about the middle. In a pull chain, the close vowels go first: bite and house break into glides, which empties the top corners, and the vowels below are drawn up into the vacancies one after another. In a push chain the open vowels go first: name raises, crowds meat, which crowds meet, which crowds bite until it has to leave the system altogether.

Switch between them and the readout tells them apart. The pull chain keeps its distance throughout, because each vowel arrives in a room that has just been vacated. The push chain spends the whole shift crowded — its closest approach is less than half the pull chain's, because every intermediate stage has a vowel sitting somewhere it does not want to be, shouldering its neighbour. Neither is proven. The crowding is the reason many linguists find the push account the harder one to believe.

What the safety margin is

Each class is four numbers: how far forward the tongue is and how high it is at the start of the vowel, then the same two for where the tongue is heading if it glides. A monophthong goes nowhere, so its last two numbers repeat its first two. Distance is then the ordinary straight-line distance in that four-dimensional space, and the safety margin is the smallest distance between any two of the twenty-one pairs. The positions here are the textbook schematic ones, not measured formants — nobody recorded Chaucer — so the margin is a number about the diagram. What it demonstrates is that the diagram is consistent: a set of movements this large can be arranged without a single collision.

Counting only the stages would be cheating: two vowels could pass close to one another on the way and the tally would never notice. The page checks the whole path. Between one stage and the next each class moves in a straight line, so the distance between any two of them is a quadratic in time, and its lowest point can be found exactly. The closest approach the pull chain ever permits is 0.270 of the chart's width. The push chain's is 0.124.

Where it finally gave way

Stage 6 is the exception, and it is worth reaching. The meat class, having spent the whole shift one step behind meet, finally caught up with it in the eighteenth century, and the two became the single vowel they are today. The distinct-vowel count drops from seven to six and the margin falls to zero. English held that contrast through two centuries of wholesale rearrangement and then lost it standing still — which is a fair summary of how sound change usually works.
