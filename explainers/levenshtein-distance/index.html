The bottom-right cell says 3, and that is the answer: kitten becomes sitting in three edits. Substitute the k for an s, substitute the e for an i, insert a g at the end. What is worth watching is not the answer but the forty-two decisions that produced it, because none of them required knowing anything about words.

Every cell is the same question

Number the rows by how much of the first word you have consumed and the columns by how much of the second. The cell at row i, column j holds one number: the cheapest way to turn the first i letters into the first j letters. The edges are free — turning nothing into sit obviously costs three insertions — so row zero and column zero just count upward.

Everything inside follows from three neighbours:

D[i][j] = min( D[i-1][j] + 1 delete a letter D[i][j-1] + 1 insert a letter D[i-1][j-1] + c substitute; c = 0 if the letters match )

Step through and watch the three outlined cells. The cell above means you already solved a slightly shorter first word, so you delete one letter to catch up. The cell to the left means you solved a shorter second word, so you insert. The diagonal means you solved both one letter shorter, and now the two letters in question either match — costing nothing — or they do not, costing one substitution. There is no fourth option, and no cell is ever revisited.

Why it has to be filled in order

The recurrence needs the cell above, the cell to the left, and the cell diagonally above-left. Fill the table left to right and top to bottom and all three are always already there. That is the whole trick of dynamic programming: not cleverness about the problem, but an order of evaluation in which nothing is ever asked for before it exists.

It also explains the cost. There are (m+1)(n+1) cells and each takes constant work, so the running time is the product of the two lengths. For two six-letter words that is forty-nine cells; for two thousand-letter genomes it is a million, which is why sequence alignment on real DNA needs something smarter.

Reading the alignment back out

The number alone does not tell you which three edits. For that, start at the bottom-right and walk backwards, at each step moving to whichever neighbour the minimum came from. The path is drawn once the table fills. A diagonal move on matching letters is a letter kept; a diagonal on different letters is a substitution; vertical and horizontal moves are a deletion and an insertion. Count the non-diagonal-match moves and you get the distance back, which is a useful check that the table and the path agree.

What to try

Set both words to abacus against cellar. They share almost nothing, and the table fills with a near-perfect diagonal staircase — the distance is six, the length of the longer word, which is the worst case. Then try flour against flower: the first three letters match, so the top-left region stays at zero along the diagonal and the whole answer is decided in the last two columns.

Finally, atlas against abacus gives four, and the back-traced path wanders rather than running straight. When two words share letters but in the wrong places, the cheapest alignment is rarely the one you would guess by eye.
