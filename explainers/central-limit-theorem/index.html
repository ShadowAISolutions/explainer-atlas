What is actually being claimed

The central limit theorem is not a claim about your data looking normal. It is a claim about one derived quantity: the mean of n independent draws. As n grows, the distribution of that mean approaches a normal distribution, whatever the population was — provided the population has a finite variance.

Two facts come for free, exactly, at every n, long before anything looks like a bell. The mean of the sample mean is the population mean:

E[X̄] = μ

and its variance shrinks in proportion to n:

Var[X̄] = σ² / n, so sd[X̄] = σ / √n

Those two lines are just linearity of expectation and independence; they need no limit theorem and no bell curve. Watch the two readouts — measured and predicted — stay locked together as you drag n. The √n is the reason quadrupling a sample only halves the error bar, and the reason surveys hit diminishing returns.

The shape argument

Convergence to the bell is a separate, deeper claim, and it is easiest to see in the third moment. Skewness of a sample mean obeys

skew[X̄ₙ] = skew[X] / √n

Choose the exponential shape and watch the skew readout: whatever lopsidedness you gave the population is divided by √n. The same happens to every higher-order lump, each one faster than the last. Normality is what remains once all the asymmetries have been washed out, and the normal distribution is precisely the shape with nothing left but a mean and a variance.

Where it fails, and how gracefully

Draw a population that is one enormous spike and one lonely far-away bar. Convergence still happens, but slowly — the distance-to-normal readout stays stubbornly high at n = 8 where the flat population was already there by n = 3. Rare extreme values dominate the mean, and you need far more of them before their influence averages out. This is not a technicality: it is why a mean daily return is a poor summary of a market that occasionally moves twenty percent.

Push it further and the theorem breaks outright. For distributions with infinite variance — the Cauchy is the standard example — the mean of n draws has exactly the same distribution as a single draw. Averaging a thousand of them buys you nothing at all. The finite-variance condition in the statement is doing real work.

What to try

Set the shape to one spike and a shelf, and step n from 1 to 32 one notch at a time. The lower panel's support widens, the bars thin out, and the distance-to-normal number falls but never quite to zero — at any finite n the sample mean is only approximately normal. The theorem is a statement about a limit, and the interesting practical question is always how close you are to it at the n you actually have.
