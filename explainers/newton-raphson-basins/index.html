To solve f(x) = 0, guess, then replace your guess with the place where the tangent line at that guess crosses zero:

xₖ₊₁ = xₖ − f(xₖ) / f′(xₖ)

On the real line with a sensible guess this is astonishingly good. Solving x² = 2 from x = 1 gives 1.5, then 1.41667, then 1.4142157, then 1.41421356237469, then the correctly rounded double. Five steps, from one significant figure to sixteen.

Why it doubles the digits

Expand f about the root r. Writing the error as eₖ = xₖ − r and keeping two terms of the Taylor series, the iteration rearranges to

eₖ₊₁ ≈ [ f″(r) / 2f′(r) ] · eₖ²

The new error is proportional to the square of the old one. An error of 10⁻⁴ becomes about 10⁻⁸, then 10⁻¹⁶. That is quadratic convergence, and it is why the number of correct digits roughly doubles per step. For x² − 2 the constant out front is 1/(2√2) ≈ 0.354, which the errors above obey to three figures once they are small enough for the expansion to hold.

The whole argument assumes you are already near r and that f′(r) is not zero. Neither assumption is free.

What the picture is

Run the same iteration in the complex plane on z³ − 1, which has three roots on the unit circle. Colour every starting point by which root it ends at. You might expect three tidy wedges. Instead you get the image above: three regions whose boundary is nowhere smooth, with copies of all three colours crowding every point of it.

The reason is that the boundary cannot belong to any one basin, and it cannot separate only two of them either. Any boundary point has all three basins arbitrarily close to it, at every scale. Zoom in as far as the control allows and the structure repeats rather than resolving. Arthur Cayley posed this problem in 1879, solved the quadratic case in a page, and never managed the cubic; the answer had to wait for Julia and Fatou forty years later.

Two ways it fails

Put the crosshair at the origin with z³ − 1 selected. There f′(0) = 0, the tangent is horizontal, and the next iterate is infinitely far away. The method has no answer, and the readout says so rather than producing a number.

Now choose z³ − 2z + 2 and set the crosshair to the origin again. Newton sends 0 to 1, and 1 straight back to 0. It is a perfect two-step cycle, and every starting point in the black region around it is caught in the same trap forever. The polynomial has three perfectly ordinary roots; the method simply never goes to look for them.

What to try

Set the zoom to about 10 and walk the real part slowly across a boundary. The iteration count flickers between low and high and the destination root changes several times inside a single step of the slider. Then watch the last readout: it retries from a point a millionth away, and near the boundary that copy lands somewhere else entirely. That is the practical warning — a root-finder that is this fast can also be this sensitive to where you set it off.
