The left pane holds a shape. The right pane holds the same shape after every one of its points has been multiplied by the matrix. Change any of the four sliders and the right-hand figure stretches, leans, spins or turns inside out — but the ratio of the two areas, sitting in the fourth readout, does not depend on what you drew. Switch from the square to the star to a scribble of your own and the number stays put. One matrix, one area factor, every shape.

That number is the determinant.

Why it is ad minus bc

Start with the unit square, the dashed outline in the left pane. Its corners are the origin, the two basis vectors, and their sum. A matrix sends the first basis vector to its first column and the second to its second, so the square is carried to the parallelogram spanned by the columns — the dashed outline on the right. Whatever area factor the matrix has, this is where you can read it off, because the square you started with had area one.

So work out that parallelogram's area. Take the rectangle that just contains it, of width a + b and height c + d, and cut away the two triangles of area bc/2, the two triangles of area ac/2, and the two of area bd/2 that lie outside the parallelogram:

(a + b)(c + d) − 2·(bc/2) − 2·(ac/2) − 2·(bd/2) = ac + ad + bc + bd − bc − ac − bd = ad − bc

Every other shape follows for free. Any region can be approximated as finely as you like by little squares; the matrix is linear, so it treats each little square exactly as it treated the unit square, scaling every one by the same factor. Add them up and the whole region has been scaled by that factor too. This is why the ratio readout ignores your drawing.

The sign is not decoration

Set a, b and d all to 1, then drag c up from zero. The determinant is 1 − c, so it falls steadily, touches zero when c reaches 1, and is negative above that — and the right-hand shape, which had been a leaning copy of the left one, is now a mirror image of it. The determinant carries both the area factor and the orientation: positive means a shape traced anticlockwise is still traced anticlockwise afterwards, negative means the plane has been turned over. The unsigned area alone cannot tell you that, which is why the shoelace formula that computes it keeps its sign rather than throwing it away.

Zero is a collapse, not a small number

Press Make it singular. It solves ad = bc for d and moves that slider there, and the right-hand pane goes flat: your shape, whatever it was, becomes a segment lying along the line through the first column. Both columns now point along that one line, so the whole plane has been pressed onto it. Area zero is exactly the statement that the columns are linearly dependent, and it is why a zero determinant means the matrix has no inverse — once everything is on a line, nothing can tell you which of the infinitely many points that arrived at a given spot was the original.

What to try

Set a = 1, b = 0, c = 0, d = 1 and then raise b alone. The determinant stays at exactly 1 no matter how far you push it: that is a shear, and a shear slides the plane sideways without compressing it. Pick the disc and watch it become a badly leaning ellipse whose area never moves off 3.142.

Then swap the two columns — put a's value into b and b's into a, and the same for c and d. The picture on the right is identical, because the parallelogram is the same set of points, but the determinant has changed sign. Order matters to orientation and not to area.

Finally, draw something wildly non-convex in the left pane — a comb, a spiral, a signature — and check the ratio again. Still ad minus bc.
