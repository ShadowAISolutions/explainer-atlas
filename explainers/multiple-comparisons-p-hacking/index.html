There is nothing to find. Both groups in every outcome are drawn from the same distribution, so every p value on screen is the product of chance alone. Step through the outcomes and the marks pile up on the left of the axis roughly uniformly, because that is what a p value does when the null hypothesis is true: it is equally likely to be anywhere between nought and one. The page checks that with a Kolmogorov and Smirnov statistic on twenty thousand simulated tests and gets 0.63 against a one per cent critical value of 1.63.

Uniform is exactly what makes twenty tests dangerous. Each has a one in twenty chance of landing under 0.05, so the chance that none of them does is 0.95 raised to the twentieth, and the chance at least one does is what is left.

chance of at least one false alarm = 1 - (1 - 0.05)^k k = 20 gives 0.6415, and the simulation over 200000 studies gives 0.6409

Two thirds. And the expected number of false alarms is 0.05 times k, which at twenty outcomes is exactly one. A study that measures twenty things and reports the one that came out significant has reported its average.

What the corrections actually buy

Bonferroni divides the threshold by the number of tests. It works: the false alarm rate over the whole family drops to 0.0488 at twenty tests, and the page asserts it stays at or under 0.05 for every k up to fifty. It is also slightly more cautious than asked, because one minus (1 - 0.05/k) to the k is always a shade under 0.05, sliding down toward 1 - e to the minus 0.05, which is 0.048771. Sidak's correction closes that gap and hits 0.05 on the nose, and under independence there is no reason to prefer Bonferroni to it.

A claim that does not survive the simulation

The brief for this page asked to assert that Benjamini and Hochberg's procedure "controls the false discovery rate below 0.05 while rejecting strictly more often than Bonferroni". The second half holds. The first does not, in the case on screen. When every hypothesis is null, every rejection is false, so the false discovery rate is just the chance of rejecting anything, and Simes showed that chance is exactly the level you set. The simulation agrees and overshoots 0.05 at several k, landing at 0.0508 for five tests. What is true is the general statement: the false discovery rate is bounded by the level times the fraction of hypotheses that are genuinely null, so it sits at 0.05 when all of them are and drops below only when some are not. Put a real effect into the first outcome and watch it drop.

The price of caution

Push the effect slider to 1.2 standard deviations and leave the sample at ten. On its own that outcome is detected 72 per cent of the time. Bury it among nineteen others and Bonferroni finds it 25 per cent of the time; Benjamini and Hochberg finds it 26. The correction did not weaken the evidence, it corrected the question you were allowed to ask of it, and that is the same trade every honest multiple testing procedure makes.

What to try

Set the effect back to zero, push outcomes to fifty, and step from one to fifty with the correction off. Watch the false alarm curve pass three quarters. Then switch to Bonferroni and step through again: the threshold line jumps left by a factor of fifty and almost nothing crosses it.
