Sensitivity Is The Least Important Number

Start where the page starts: one person in a hundred has the condition, the test catches ninety-nine per cent of them, and it clears ninety-five per cent of everyone else. Those are respectable numbers. A positive result means a one-in-six chance of having the condition. Five out of every six people who get the bad news do not have it.

The picture above is why. Of ten thousand people, a hundred have it and ninety-nine of those test positive. The other nine thousand nine hundred do not have it, and five per cent of them — four hundred and ninety-five people — test positive anyway. The positive pile holds ninety-nine real cases and four hundred and ninety-five false alarms, because the pool of healthy people is so much larger that even a small slice of it swamps the whole diseased column.

The dial that does nothing

Now try to reach the ninety per cent target. Push sensitivity to one hundred: the answer moves from 16.67 per cent to 16.81. That is the entire available effect of the number printed on the box, because sensitivity can only ever range from about a half to one, so it can at most double your odds. Every other lever on the page is unbounded.

posterior odds = prior odds × sensitivity / (1 − specificity)

Written as odds rather than probabilities, the whole thing is one multiplication, and it shows which factors have room to move. Sensitivity sits in the numerator with a range of two. The false alarm rate sits in the denominator and can go from one in five to one in a thousand, a range of two hundred. And the prior odds — the base rate — span whatever the world hands you. This is why the page is built as a target to hit: the tuning teaches the asymmetry faster than the formula states it.

Two levers, one effect

Because the odds form is a product, doubling the prior odds and halving the false alarm rate do exactly the same thing to the answer. Not approximately: identically. Testing a group twice as likely to be affected is worth precisely as much as building a test that raises a false alarm half as often, and the second is usually far more expensive than the first. That is the argument for testing people who have a reason to be tested, rather than everybody, and it is also why the same test can be excellent in a clinic and useless as a screen.

What a second test buys you

The multiplication also tells you what to do when you cannot change any of the three numbers: test again. A second independent positive multiplies the odds by the same factor a second time, so the one-in-six becomes very nearly four in five. This is exactly how confirmatory testing works, and it is why a screening programme that never confirms a positive is doing harm rather than good. The catch is the word independent — two runs of the same test on the same sample usually fail together, and then the second result multiplies nothing.

What to try

Leave the base rate at one in a hundred and try to reach ninety per cent using sensitivity alone: you cannot, at any setting. Then drag specificity from 95 to 99.9 and watch the answer cross the line on its own. Finally set specificity back to 95 and drag the base rate instead — one in five gets you there too. Two very different interventions, the same arithmetic, and the number on the box was never involved in either.
