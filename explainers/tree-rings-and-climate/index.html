Sliding One Log Along Another

A tree ring is a year, and how wide it is records that year's growing season. Slide an undated timber along a chronology whose dates are already known, and when the pattern of wide and narrow years locks, the timber is dated — to the year, centuries before anyone kept records.

The brown bars are a chronology: a series of ring widths whose dates are already known, built by working backwards from living trees. The green bars are a core drilled out of a roof beam with no documents attached. Both trees stood through the same weather, so both recorded the same run of good and bad summers, and the only question is which of the chronology's years the beam's rings belong to. Slide it until the pattern lines up. When it locks, every ring in the beam has a year on it.

Growth first

Raw widths cannot be compared directly, because a young tree lays down wide rings whatever the weather and a old one lays down narrow ones. That trend is the loudest thing in the series and it belongs to the tree, not the climate. Set detrending to none and watch the correlation curve turn into a single broad hump with no peak worth the name: two growth curves agree with each other everywhere, so the pattern that carries the dates is drowned.

The standard fix is to fit a falling growth curve to each series and divide, which leaves a ratio near one with the climate riding on it. Subtracting a straight line instead is cruder and has one exact property worth knowing: least squares guarantees that the residuals have a fitted slope of zero, to machine precision, every time. Dividing by a curve guarantees nothing of the kind, which is why the first thing anyone checks after detrending is whether a trend is still in there.

The lock

With good wood the lock is unmistakable. At a bad-wood setting of 0.1 the correlation at the true offset beats every other offset by at least 0.345, over three hundred seeded pairs of cores, every single time, with a median margin of 0.554. You do not have to judge it; the number does the judging.

Now drag bad wood to the right. At 0.2 the method still finds the right year on every pair, but the margin's worst case has fallen to 0.08. At 0.3 it is right 95 times in a hundred and the margin's fifth percentile is 0.009 — a hair. At 0.45 it is right 46 times in a hundred, and at 0.6, fourteen.

The cliff

That is not a method degrading. That is a method falling off a cliff, and the way it falls is the dangerous part. Past the cliff the highest correlation is usually at the wrong offset, and it is a perfectly respectable-looking correlation. The margin readout goes negative: the true year is no longer the best match, so there is nothing on the chart to tell you that you have just dated a beam to a century it never stood in. Cross dating does not fail by returning "unknown". It fails by returning a confident wrong answer.

This is why real dendrochronology never accepts a single core. Several cores from the same timber, several timbers from the same building, and every one of them has to land on the same year against the same chronology. Agreement between independent pieces of wood is doing the work that a single correlation cannot do. Shorten the core to thirty rings and the same 0.3 setting that was right 95 times in a hundred is right 31 times in a hundred: there is less pattern to match, so there is less to separate the right answer from the best of the wrong ones.

Missing rings

A tree in a drought year can lay down no measurable ring at all. Tick lose a ring and the count of rings stops being a count of years: every ring inward of the missing one is now dated a year too late, all the way to the pith. The pattern match still works, because the match is made on the outer part where nothing is missing, and it is precisely the disagreement between the ring count and the matched date that reveals the gap. A ring count alone would never have found it.

What a narrow ring says

Rather less than people want it to. A narrow ring is a bad growing season for that tree, and the reason can be drought, cold, a late frost, shade from a neighbour that fell three years later, or insects. The climate signal is only the part shared by many trees on many sites, which is exactly the part that survives averaging and exactly the part that makes cross dating possible in the first place. One narrow ring in one core is an anecdote. The same narrow ring in forty cores is a year.

What to try

Set bad wood to 0.5, press find the best match, and compare the offset it returns with the true offset in the readouts. Do it a few times with fell another tree. Then shorten the core to 30 rings and watch how much sooner the cliff arrives. Then set detrending to none at a bad wood of 0.1, where the method was otherwise perfect, and watch it drop to right roughly a third of the time.
