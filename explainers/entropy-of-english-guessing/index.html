The passage above is ordinary English you have not read. One letter is hidden and you are asked to name it. If you are wrong you are asked again, and again, until you get it, and the only thing being recorded is how many attempts it took. That is the whole of Shannon's 1951 experiment. He was not interested in your guesses. He was interested in the numbers.

Why the guess numbers are the measurement

Suppose you and a copy of yourself both know English equally well. Hand the copy the list of guess numbers and the text you have already seen, and it can rebuild the passage: make the same first guess, and if the number says three, make the second and third guesses too. The list of numbers is therefore a complete encoding of the text, so the text cannot carry more information than the numbers do. That gives an upper bound straight away, and a matching lower bound follows if the guesser always tries the most likely letter first:

lower = sum over i of i (q(i) - q(i+1)) log2 i upper = sum over i of -q(i) log2 q(i)

where q(i) is the fraction of characters you got on the i-th attempt. With a flat alphabet of twenty six letters and a space every character would cost log2(27), which is 4.75 bits. Counting how often each letter actually appears in this text brings it down to 4.03. Letting a model see the previous letter brings it to 3.16, and two letters brings it to 2.31.

Where those numbers stop meaning anything

Keep going and the sequence falls off a cliff: 1.53 bits at three letters of context, 0.98 at four, 0.63 at five. It is tempting to read that as English getting more predictable. It is not. The counts come from thirteen thousand characters, and there are more than half a million possible four letter contexts, so by the fourth order almost every context in the text occurs exactly once and the model is simply reciting what it was shown. The upper panel makes this visible. The second line is the same measurement taken on text the model was never fitted to, and it does not fall forever: it bottoms out at two letters of context and then climbs, and by five letters it is worse than knowing nothing about context at all. That turning point sits at two whatever smoothing you choose, which the page checks at four settings.

So the honest reading of the ladder is that the falling numbers measure two different things at once, and only one of them is English. Shannon's own figure of roughly one bit per character came from human guessers, who bring the whole language with them: vocabulary, grammar, the subject of the paragraph, and the knowledge that a sentence has to end somewhere. No table of letter counts is going to find that in thirteen thousand characters, and this page does not pretend otherwise.

What the spare bits are for

If a character costs one bit rather than 4.75, then something like three quarters of written English is redundant, and that redundancy is what makes it readable at all. It is why you can read a page with the vowels removed, why a smudged word is still legible, and why crosswords are possible. It is also exactly what a compressor eats: a file of English text squeezes to a fraction of its size because the letters were never independent, and the compressor is doing formally the same job as you are doing above, predicting the next character and paying only for its surprise.

What to try

Play twenty characters by hand with the context slider at two and watch your own bounds settle. You will find the spaces and the ends of common words come first time and the first letter of a new word almost never does, which is the shape of the whole result. Expect the bracket to sit too low at first: a dozen characters give a guess number distribution that is far too tidy, and both bounds are entropies of that distribution, so both come out as underestimates until the tail fills in. Then press the model button a few times at each context length and watch it get worse, not better, past two.
