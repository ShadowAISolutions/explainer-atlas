Every Interval Slightly Wrong, On Purpose

A perfect fifth is two notes whose frequencies stand in the ratio 3 to 2. The fifth on a piano is not that. The mismatch is small, deliberate, and the reason you can play in any key.

The just ratio is the small whole-number one. Equal temperament replaces it with 2 to the power n over 12.

Two notes sound consonant when their frequencies share small whole-number ratios, because then their harmonics land on top of each other instead of near each other. A perfect fifth is 3:2 — three times the lower note's frequency is exactly twice the upper's, and that shared harmonic rings as a single clean tone.

Miss the ratio slightly and the two harmonics are no longer the same frequency. They drift in and out of phase, and their sum swells and fades at the difference between them. That is beating, and the ear hears it as roughness rather than as two pitches.

Why not simply use the ratios

Because they do not close. Stack twelve perfect fifths and you land almost, but not quite, seven octaves up:

(3/2)¹² = 129.746… 2⁷ = 128 difference = 23.46 cents

That leftover is the Pythagorean comma, and it is nearly a quarter of a semitone — easily audible. A keyboard tuned in pure fifths starting from C has a D sharp and an E flat that are different notes. Historical tunings dealt with this by hiding the error: they made most keys sweet and one interval, the wolf, unusable. You simply did not write in that key.

Equal temperament makes a different trade. Define the semitone as the twelfth root of two and build everything from it:

ratio(n) = 2^(n/12), cents(f₁,f₂) = 1200 · log₂(f₂/f₁)

Now twelve fifths close exactly, because seven twelfths times twelve is exactly seven. The comma has not disappeared; it has been spread evenly, 1.955 cents taken off every fifth. Nothing is pure and nothing is unusable.

Where the error actually lands

Read the bar chart. The fifth and fourth are off by under two cents, which is below what most listeners notice on a sustained tone. The thirds and sixths are the casualties: the major third is nearly fourteen cents sharp, the minor third sixteen cents flat. Select the major third at 440 Hz and look at the beat rate — around seventeen beats a second, fast enough to sound like a buzz rather than a pulse. That is why an equal-tempered major third sounds busy next to a barbershop chord, where singers bend to the pure 5:4 with no fixed keyboard to stop them.

The cents unit is what makes this comparable. A cent is a hundredth of an equal-tempered semitone, defined logarithmically so that equal ratios give equal cents at any pitch. An interval 14 cents sharp is 14 cents sharp in every octave, even though its error in hertz grows with pitch.

What to try

Set the interval to the perfect fifth and widen the window to four seconds: one slow swell, about one and a half per second at A440. Now switch to the major third without touching anything else — the envelope becomes a blur. Then drag the root down an octave to 220 Hz and watch every beat rate halve, while the cents error does not move at all. The error is a fixed proportion; only its expression in hertz depends on where you are on the keyboard.
