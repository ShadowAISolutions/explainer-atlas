The top tenth of this class averaged about 1.75 standard deviations above the mean in the first round. In the second they average about 0.88. Nothing happened in between. No one was coached, nothing was taught, the test was the same difficulty. The group simply is not as far ahead as it looked, and it never was.

Half of being top is being lucky

Treat a score as durable skill plus the day you happened to have. Choosing the highest scorers selects for both at once: they are genuinely good, and they also caught a following wind. Skill turns up again next week. Luck does not. So the group keeps the part that was real and loses the part that was not, and the average slides back toward the middle.

That is the whole mechanism, and it is not a tendency or a pressure. It is arithmetic. For scores standardised to mean zero and correlation ρ between rounds, the expected second score of anyone who scored x the first time is exactly ρx. Set the reliability slider to 1 and the fallback vanishes, because there is no luck left to lose. Set it to 0 and the whole lead evaporates.

Exactly how far they fall

Selecting the top fraction q by the first score, the group's first-round average is the height of the bell at the cutoff divided by the fraction kept:

cutoff z = the point with q of the class above it round 1 mean = φ(z) / q ( = 1.755 for the top 10% ) round 2 mean = ρ · φ(z) / q the fall = (1 − ρ) · φ(z) / q

Both factors do what you would expect. Choose a narrower slice and the group starts further out, so it has further to fall. Use a flakier test and it keeps less of its lead. Neither factor knows anything about the pupils, which is the point: the fall is a property of how they were chosen, not of them.

How this manufactures results

Now read it as an evaluation. Pick the worst-performing schools and send in support; pick the patients with the highest blood pressure and treat them; pick the players in the worst slump and change their technique. Measure again afterwards and every one of them improves. Push the coaching slider up and watch the naive before-and-after number: it reports the real effect plus the fallback, and with the effect at zero it still reports the fallback on its own.

The fix is in the design rather than in the statistics. Draw up the shortlist, then toss a coin for each name on it: half get the coaching, half get nothing. The two halves are equally extreme, so they fall back by exactly the same amount, and the difference between their two changes is the real effect and nothing else. That is what the control readout does, and it stays near zero however hard the selection is cranked. Taking the next-best pupils as a comparison would not do — they start lower, so they fall less, and the gap reappears.

What to try

Set your guess where you genuinely expect the second round to land before touching reveal. Most people put it near where the group started. Then drag the reliability to 0.9 and guess again: the fall is now small, and a study on a reliable measure is much harder to fool with. Finally set the coaching to +0.5, reveal, and compare the naive verdict with the control comparison — one of them is right.
