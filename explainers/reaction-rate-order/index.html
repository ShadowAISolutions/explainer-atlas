What the order actually is

A reaction's rate is how fast the reactant disappears, and for a very large class of reactions that rate depends on the concentration through a single power:

d[A]/dt = −k [A]ⁿ

The exponent n is the order. It is not the stoichiometric coefficient in the balanced equation, it cannot be deduced from the formula, and it is frequently not a whole number — vial D here is order 1.5, which is what you get when a step in the mechanism involves a species in equilibrium with A. The order is a summary of the mechanism, and the only way to it is measurement.

Integrating that equation gives the curve drawn through the points. For n = 1 it is an exponential; for every other n it is a power:

n = 1: [A] = [A]₀ e^(−kt) n ≠ 1: [A]^(1−n) = [A]₀^(1−n) − (1−n) k t

Read it off the half-lives

The two half-life readouts are the fastest diagnostic on the page. Set the order to 1 and drag the rate constant: both readouts move together and stay equal. That is the signature of first order — 0.693/k, with no mention of concentration in it, which is why radioactive decay has a half-life you can print on a chart and a second-order reaction does not.

Now set the order to 2. The second half-life is exactly twice the first, because for n = 2 the half-life is 1/(k[A]₀) and the concentration has halved. Vial B takes 2.75 minutes to lose its first half and 5.49 to lose the next. Set the order to 0 and it inverts: each half-life is half the one before, because a zero-order reaction chews through reactant at a flat rate regardless of how much is left, and simply stops when there is none. Vial C runs out entirely at 21.4 minutes.

Or read it off the straight line

The three small panels on the right are the method that predates curve fitting and still gets taught first. Plot the measurements three ways — concentration, its logarithm, its reciprocal — and whichever comes out straight names the order, because each is exactly the integrated rate law for n = 0, 1 and 2 rearranged into y = mx + c. The panel with the highest R² is marked in green.

On vial A, ln[A] scores 0.990 while the other two sit at 0.91 and 0.90. On vial C the plain concentration scores 0.9975. On vial D — the order-1.5 one — the first- and second-order panels come out at 0.983 and 0.982, a tie, and that tie is itself the finding: no integer order fits, so the order is somewhere between. That is the case the linearised plots handle badly and the fitted curve handles well, which is why chemists now do both.

What to try

Open vial C and leave the order at 1. Press Snap k to the best value and look at the fit gauge: the best first-order law is still four times worse than the noise. Then move the order to 0 and press it again. The gauge fills and the residual drops onto the noise floor, which is where a correct model should land — matching the data better than the noise floor would mean you were fitting the measurement error, not the chemistry.

Then open vial D and try every order in turn. Nothing reaches the floor until you pass 1.4, and the best fit is at 1.5. Press Reveal this vial to check.
