Your Interval Either Contains It Or It Does Not

A ninety-five per cent interval is not ninety-five per cent likely to hold the true value. It came out of a recipe that succeeds ninety-five times in a hundred. Draw a population, run the recipe a hundred times, and count the failures.

Every horizontal bar in the lower panel is one confidence interval, built from its own fresh sample out of the population above it. The vertical line is the population mean, which you can see because this is a simulation and the population is something you drew. Count the bars that fail to reach the line. At ninety-five per cent, on a bell-shaped population, about five of the hundred will miss.

That is the whole meaning of the number. Ninety-five per cent describes how often the procedure works, taken over all the samples it might have drawn. It says nothing about the one interval in front of you. That interval either contains the mean or it does not; there is no probability left in it once the sample is in hand. The uncertainty lives in the sampling, and you spent it when you sampled.

Where the width comes from

interval = sample mean ± t × s / √n

The sample mean wanders around the population mean with a spread of σ/√n, so an interval reaching out a couple of those wanderings in each direction catches the mean most of the time. Push the sample size up and every bar shortens by exactly the factor √n demands: quadruple the sample and the interval halves. That is a punishing exchange rate, and it is why the last decimal place of precision is so expensive.

The multiplier is where the two recipes differ. If you already know the population spread you can use a fixed number from the normal distribution — 1.96 for ninety-five per cent. You almost never know it, so you estimate it from the same small sample, and that estimate is itself noisy. Student’s t multiplier compensates by being larger, and by shrinking toward 1.96 as the sample grows. At three observations it is 4.30; at forty it is 2.02. Switch the recipe to the z interval at a sample size of three and watch the failures pile up: you are pretending to know something you do not.

Where the promise breaks

Choose the strongly skewed population and set the sample size to five. The bars still say ninety-five per cent, but only about eighty-eight in a hundred reach the line. The t multiplier is derived on the assumption that the population is normal, and a long right tail breaks it in a specific way: most samples miss the rare large values entirely, so they under-estimate both the mean and the spread at once, and the interval is both too low and too short. The failures are lopsided — nearly all of them fall below the mean rather than above it.

Now walk the sample size up. At ten the coverage is about ninety per cent, at thirty about ninety-three, and it keeps creeping toward the promise. This is the central limit theorem arriving late: the sample mean becomes normal eventually whatever the population looks like, but how late it arrives depends on how skewed that population is. The usual advice that thirty is enough is a rule of thumb about mildly skewed data, not a theorem.

What to try

Draw a population with one narrow spike and a long low tail off to the right, then set the sample size to four and read the long-run coverage: a nominal ninety-five can land in the seventies. Then set the confidence level to ninety-nine and watch what it costs — the bars get a third longer and the recipe still under-delivers, because the problem is the shape, not the level. Finally flatten the population out and see the coverage snap back: a symmetric population is forgiving at almost any sample size.
