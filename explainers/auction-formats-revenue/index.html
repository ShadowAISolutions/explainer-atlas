Why you tell the truth in a second-price auction

You win if your bid is highest, and you pay the second-highest bid. Your bid therefore decides only whether you win, never how much you pay. Bid above your value and the only auctions you newly win are ones where the price lands between your value and your bid — you win and lose money. Bid below it and the only auctions you newly lose are ones you would have won at a profit. Every deviation is weakly bad against every possible set of rival bids, which is stronger than an equilibrium: you do not need to know or guess anything about the others. That property is why the format is used for advertising slots and for spectrum, and it is what William Vickrey got the Nobel for.

Why you shade in a first-price auction

Here you pay what you bid, so bidding your value guarantees a payoff of exactly zero. You must shade, and how much depends on how many rivals you are shading against. With n bidders whose values are uniform, the symmetric equilibrium is

b(v) = v · (n − 1) / n

which is a bidder's own value multiplied by the probability-weighted expectation of the highest rival value, given they are the highest. Two bidders shade to half. Ten bidders shade to ninety per cent, because with nine rivals the chance that one of them is close behind you is high. Drag the bidder slider and watch the gold histogram slide right towards the blue one without ever reaching it.

The revenue is the same, and it is no accident

The seller's expected take in the second-price auction is the expected second-highest value, which for n uniform draws is (n−1)/(n+1). In the first-price auction the seller takes the winner's bid, which is (n−1)/n times the expected highest value, and the expected highest of n uniform draws is n/(n+1). The n cancels and you get (n−1)/(n+1) again.

That is not a coincidence of the uniform distribution. The revenue equivalence theorem says any auction that always gives the item to the highest-value bidder and gives a bidder with the lowest possible value zero expected payoff raises the same expected revenue, whatever the bidding rules look like. The format changes who pays what in any single auction — the two running averages wobble differently, and at 200 auctions they can be several per cent apart — but not the average.

What a reserve price actually does

Push the reserve to 0.50 and revenue goes up in both formats. A reserve is the one lever that beats revenue equivalence, because it breaks the first condition: the item no longer always goes to the highest-value bidder. When every value falls below the reserve the sale simply does not happen, and the surplus that a buyer would have enjoyed is destroyed rather than transferred.

Myerson's result gives the exact trade. With uniform values the expected revenue at reserve r is

R(r) = (n−1)/(n+1) − 2n·r^(n+1)/(n+1) + r^n

and differentiating gives n·r^(n−1)·(1 − 2r), so the best reserve is exactly one half — for two bidders and for two hundred. That is worth sitting with: the optimal reserve does not depend on how many bidders show up, only on the distribution their values come from.

What to try

Set two bidders and a reserve of 0.50. Revenue rises from 0.333 to 0.417, a quarter more, while the item goes unsold a quarter of the time. Then set ten bidders and keep the reserve at 0.50: revenue barely moves, because with ten draws it is nearly certain that someone clears half anyway. The reserve is worth a lot in a thin market and almost nothing in a thick one, which is the practical form of the result.

Finally, drop the runs to 100 and press New draw a few times. The two formats disagree by whole percentage points, in both directions. Revenue equivalence is a statement about expectations, and expectations are not what any particular seller gets.
