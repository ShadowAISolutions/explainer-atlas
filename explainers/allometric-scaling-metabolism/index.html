A Mouse Is Not A Small Elephant

Geometry says a body's heat loss should follow its surface, and so metabolism should go as mass to the two thirds. Fifteen published mammals spanning five decades of size say otherwise. Drag the exponent until the line lies flat through them and read what you get.

An elephant is about 182,500 times the mass of a mouse. If a body were a furnace whose output scaled with its bulk, the elephant would burn 182,500 times as much. The fifteen mammals in the table say 13,500 times. Something between mass and metabolism is losing four orders of magnitude, and the question is what exponent describes the loss.

The argument that fails

Heat leaves through skin. Skin is area, and area on a shape held to fixed proportions goes as the square of a length while mass goes as the cube, so area goes as mass to the two thirds. A resting animal must shed heat as fast as it makes it, so metabolic rate ought to follow area: an exponent of exactly 0.667. This is clean, physical, and about a century of measurement disagrees with it.

Set the exponent to 0.667 and switch the plate to residuals. The points do not scatter around the line, they ramp across it: the mouse sits 0.271 of a decade below and the elephant 0.353 above, and the correlation between residual and body mass is 0.931. That climb is the signature of a slope that is too shallow. A wrong exponent never looks like noise. It looks like a tilt.

What the data gives instead

log(metabolic rate) = 1.8328 + 0.7618 × log(body mass)

Least squares on the fifteen mammals returns an exponent of 0.7618, with the line accounting for 99.76 per cent of the variance across five and a quarter decades of mass. It is 0.0118 away from three quarters and 0.0951 away from two thirds — eight times nearer the quarter power. Forcing the slope to 0.75 costs almost nothing: typical error rises from 12.3 to 13.0 per cent. Forcing it to 0.667 costs a great deal: 37.4 per cent, with the tilt above.

Two thirds and three quarters sound close, and over one decade of mass they are. Over the range in this table they diverge by a factor of 2.74, which is the difference between a plausible ration for a horse and a starving one. The largest single miss the fitted line makes is the elephant, high by a factor of 1.33 — that datum comes from a single animal measured in 1936, and it is the one number here a careful reader should hold loosely.

What a quarter power does to time

Divide both sides by mass. Metabolic rate per kilogram goes as mass to the minus 0.238, so a gram of mouse burns 13.5 times as hard as a gram of elephant. Tissue does not have one metabolic speed; it has the speed its owner's size permits.

Switch the quantity to heart rate. The fitted exponent is −0.2501, within 0.0001 of minus a quarter, which is what it must be if each beat moves a volume of blood proportional to body size and the blood has to carry that metabolism. Switch to lifespan and the exponent is +0.2374, the same quarter with the sign flipped. Multiply a rate of minus a quarter by a duration of plus a quarter and the exponents cancel: a lifetime's heartbeats should not depend on size at all.

Where the tidy story breaks

It nearly does not. Fit lifetime beats against mass directly and the exponent is −0.0126, flat enough that the trend predicts only a 14 per cent fall from mouse to elephant, with an R-squared of 0.005 — mass explains essentially nothing. But flat is not constant. The actual spread across these fifteen is a factor of 5.33, from a sheep at 5.52 × 108 beats to a human at 2.95 × 109, so the often-repeated claim that mammals get about a billion beats to within a factor of three is false on this table.

It fails for a reason worth knowing. Drop the three primates and the remaining twelve span 2.14, comfortably inside a factor of three, from the sheep up to the cat. Primates average 3.44 times more lifetime beats than non-primates of any size. The quarter-power machinery is intact; primates simply live far longer than their metabolism buys them, and a human most of all.

What to try

Put the mouse on the left and the elephant on the right, then drag the exponent from 0.5 to 1.0 and watch the line pivot through the middle of the data. Press Surface area, switch to residuals, and look at the ramp. Press Least squares and watch it flatten to nothing — the fitted slope is the one that leaves no tilt, which is all least squares means. Then set the quantity to beats in a lifetime and find the three points sitting above the rest.

Figures are representative published values for resting metabolic rate, resting heart rate and maximum recorded lifespan, rounded from the standard compilations (Kleiber 1947; Brody 1945; Schmidt-Nielsen 1984). The fitted exponents are robust to the exact choices; the elephant is the least certain entry.
