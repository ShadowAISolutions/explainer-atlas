Which Number Did the Poison Move?

An enzyme's behaviour is two numbers: how fast it can possibly go, and how much substrate it needs to get halfway there. Add an inhibitor and one of them changes. Which one tells you how the inhibitor works.

The curve on the left bends over because an enzyme is a finite number of machines. Give it more substrate and it works faster, until every machine is busy and more substrate changes nothing. Two numbers describe the whole shape: the ceiling, and the substrate concentration that reaches half of it.

v = Vmax * S / (Km + S) at S = Km v = Vmax / 2 exactly at S much less than Km v = (Vmax/Km) * S Vmax/Km is the enzyme's efficiency on scarce substrate

The diagnosis is which number holds still

Switch the inhibitor and push the dose. A competitive inhibitor occupies the same site as the substrate, so enough substrate always wins: the ceiling is untouched and only the half-way point moves out. A non-competitive inhibitor binds somewhere else and stops the machine from working, so no amount of substrate rescues it: the ceiling falls and the half-way point stays. An uncompetitive inhibitor binds only to the enzyme that already has substrate in it, which sounds obscure but has the neatest signature of the three — the ceiling and the half-way point fall by exactly the same factor, so their ratio, the efficiency, never moves at all.

Those are three different invariants, and the panel at the top names whichever one is holding. That is the actual laboratory workflow: you do not measure a mechanism, you measure two numbers under several doses and notice which one refuses to budge.

Why the straight line is a trap

Turning the curve into a straight line by plotting one over the rate against one over the substrate is the classical trick, and the right-hand panel shows why it is seductive: competitive inhibition gives lines through a common point on the vertical axis, non-competitive gives lines through a common point on the horizontal axis, uncompetitive gives parallel lines. The mechanism is visible at a glance.

Now push the noise slider. The reciprocal of a small number is a large number with a large error, so the measurements at low substrate — the least reliable ones, where the rate is smallest — are thrown to the far right of the plot and dominate the fit. Over two hundred simulated repeats the double-reciprocal method misses the ceiling by about thirteen per cent while fitting the curve directly misses it by three, and it misses the half-way point by a third rather than a ninth. The page measures both and asserts the gap.

What to try

Set the noise to zero and step the dose from 0 to 5 under each inhibitor in turn, watching only the top panel. Then set the inhibitor to none, put the noise at 12 per cent, and drag the repeat slider: the curve on the left barely twitches while the straight line on the right pivots wildly. Same data, same enzyme, one method that survives measurement error and one that does not.
