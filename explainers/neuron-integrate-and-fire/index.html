Below This Current, Nothing Happens

A neuron is a leaky bucket with a trip switch. Turn the tap down far enough and the switch never trips — not slowly, not rarely, but never.

The five knobs

2.00 nA

The steady injected current. Arrow keys step by 0.01 nA; Home and End jump to the ends.

10.0 MΩ

How much voltage a given current buys. Current times resistance is where the voltage is heading.

10.0 ms

How fast it gets there. Changes the rate but never whether the neuron fires at all.

15.0 mV

How far above rest the trip switch sits. This is the knob that moves the cliff.

2.0 ms

Dead time held at reset after each spike. Sets the ceiling the rate can never pass.

120 ms

How much recorded time the left pane shows. Display only — the neuron ignores it.

The green trace on the left is a voltage, and it is doing the only thing a leaky membrane can do: heading for wherever the injected current is pushing it, at a rate set by how leaky it is. What makes it a neuron rather than a resistor is the dashed red line. The moment the voltage touches it, the cell fires, the voltage is slammed back to rest, and the climb starts over. Everything interesting about this page follows from one consequence of that arrangement: if the voltage is not heading somewhere above the dashed line, it never arrives, and the cell is silent forever.

A bucket with a trip switch

Between spikes the membrane is a resistor and a capacitor in parallel, fed by a current. Measuring voltage from the resting potential, that is one line of physics:

τ dV/dt = −V + IR   with   τ = RC

The voltage is always moving toward IR, closing whatever gap remains at a fractional rate of one part in τ per millisecond. Starting from the reset value Vr, the solution is an exponential approach that never overshoots its target:

V(t) = IR + (Vr − IR) e−t/τ

Set that equal to the threshold Vth and solve for the time it takes, then add the refractory dead time tref the cell spends pinned at reset after firing. The whole behaviour of the neuron is in this one expression:

ISI = tref + τ ln[ (IR − Vr) / (IR − Vth) ]

At the settings this page opens with, IR is 20 mV, threshold is 15 mV above rest, and τ is 10 ms, so the climb takes 10 ln 4 = 13.86 ms, the interval is 15.86 ms, and the cell fires at 63 Hz.

The cliff is real, and it is not a discontinuity

Look at the logarithm. Its argument blows up as IR falls toward Vth, and at IR = Vth the neuron's target voltage is exactly the threshold, which an exponential approaches but never reaches. Below that the target is under the line entirely. So there is a current with a name — the rheobase, Irh = Vth/R — beneath which the output is not merely slow but exactly zero, for any length of recording you care to make. Notice which knobs move it: threshold and resistance do, the time constant does not. Making a cell slower changes how often it fires, never whether it fires.

The tempting conclusion is that the firing rate jumps at the rheobase. It does not. As the current approaches Irh from above, the interval grows without bound and the rate slides continuously to zero — but it does so logarithmically, which means every derivative of the rate vanishes there too. The curve leaves the axis infinitely flatly. That is why on any real plot, and on the right-hand pane here, the onset reads as a wall: the rate is genuinely continuous and genuinely unmeasurable over the same stretch of current.

The ceiling

At the other end the refractory period takes over. However hard you drive the cell, the climb time can only shrink toward zero, so the interval can only shrink toward tref and the rate is capped at 1/tref — 500 Hz at the default 2 ms. Set the refractory period to zero and the ceiling disappears: the rate then grows without limit, roughly linearly in current, because for large IR the logarithm flattens to Vth/IR.

What to try

Start by dragging the current down from 2 nA. The trace stays regular and simply slows, then around 1.6 nA it slows alarmingly, and at 1.50 nA the spikes stop dead. Press the rheobase button to land exactly on that edge and watch the voltage crawl up and stall a hair under the line. Now, without touching the current, raise the resistance: more volts per nanoamp moves the target back above threshold and the cell starts again. Finally set the refractory period to zero and push the current to 4 nA — the readout sails past 500 Hz, which is the model honestly telling you that a leaky integrator with no dead time is not a neuron.
