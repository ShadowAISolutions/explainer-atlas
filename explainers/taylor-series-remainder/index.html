The red curve is a polynomial. It is glued to the black curve at the centre, marked by the open circle, and there it agrees in value, in slope, in curvature, and in as many further derivatives as you have kept terms. Away from the centre it drifts. The gold band is how far it is allowed to drift: the Lagrange bound, computed from the function alone, before the polynomial has been evaluated anywhere at all. The red curve never leaves the band. Along the axis, the green segment marks where the band is thinner than the accuracy you asked for.

The bound you can compute first

Truncating after degree n leaves a remainder, and Lagrange's theorem says that remainder is not a vague error term but the same shape as one more term of the series, with the derivative evaluated at some unknown point between the centre and where you stand. You never learn that point, and you do not need it: replace the derivative there by the largest it gets anywhere on the interval and the equality becomes an inequality you can actually evaluate.

|R(x)| <= max |f super (n+1)| * |x - a| super (n+1) / (n+1)!

Two things do the work and they pull opposite ways. The power punishes distance from the centre. The factorial rewards terms, and it outruns anything the numerator can manage — provided the derivatives stay bounded. For sine they never exceed one, so the bound is the bare power over the factorial. For ex they grow, but only as fast as ex itself, and the factorial still wins.

This is not a loose safety margin. Bring the probe in towards the centre and the ratio of the real error to the bound climbs towards one: at 0.05 away it already sits between 0.958 and 0.967, and it cannot fall below 0.951, because the only slack in the whole argument is the gap between the derivative at the unknown point and the derivative at the far end.

Doubling the degree does not square the error

The factorial makes it tempting to think twice the terms squares your accuracy. For ex about zero at x = 1 it does, comfortably. At x = 0.01 it does not: the degree-2 error is 1.7e-7, its square is 2.8e-14, and the degree-4 error is 8.3e-13, thirty times larger. The crossing sits where the distance equals ((n+1)!)2 over (2n+1)!, which for two terms is 0.300, and the measured ratio is 1.10 at x = 0.25 and 0.65 at x = 0.40.

The reason is a mismatch of currencies. Doubling the degree buys one further power of the distance and a far larger factorial. Squaring the error demands n+1 further powers of the distance. Where the distance is small, powers of it are worth more than factorials, and squaring is the more expensive request.

The wall that more terms cannot cross

Switch to 1/(1-x) or to ln(1+x) and the ladder on the right stops climbing. These functions have a singularity a fixed distance from the centre, and their derivatives grow just fast enough to cancel the factorial exactly at that distance. Inside it the polynomial converges: at x = 0.8 the error falls from 1.3 to 5.0e-3 between degree 5 and degree 30. Outside it the polynomial diverges. At x = 1.2 the error rises at every single step of that same sweep, from 15 to 1400. Adding terms there is not merely wasted, it is actively making the answer worse. Move the centre and the wall moves with it, which is what the centre slider is for.

What to try

Leave the function at ex, set the accuracy to 1e-6, and walk the degree up with Add a term. The half-width the bound guarantees goes from 0.0014 at one term to 0.159 at four, 1.72 at twelve and 3.76 at twenty: sixteen extra terms buy twenty-four times the reach, not sixteen times. Then switch to 1/(1-x), drag the probe out past the green, and press Add a term repeatedly. Watch the red curve pull away from the black one rather than towards it, and watch the ledger's running total march off in the wrong direction while every individual term still looks perfectly reasonable.
