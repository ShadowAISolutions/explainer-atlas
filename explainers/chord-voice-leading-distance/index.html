Between Two Chords

Two chords sound smooth in succession when their notes move a short total distance. That distance is not a matter of taste. It is the answer to a small optimisation problem, and you can watch it being solved.

Play a C major triad and then a G major triad. If every finger jumps to the nearest available note the change is almost inaudible as motion — it sounds like the harmony shifted underneath a held sound. If instead each finger leaps to the note a fifth above, the same two chords sound like two separate events. Same chords, same order, different experience, and the only thing that changed is which note went where.

That choice has a name and a number. A voice leading is an assignment of the notes of one chord to the notes of the next, one to one. Its size is the total number of semitones the voices travel, each voice by the shorter way round the twelve-note circle. The smallest size any assignment achieves is the voice-leading distance between the two chords.

The metric on the circle

Pitch classes live on a circle of twelve, so the distance between two of them is the shorter of the two arcs:

d(a, b) = min( (a − b) mod 12 , (b − a) mod 12 ) = 6 − |6 − ((a − b) mod 12)|

Nothing is ever more than six semitones from anything, because at six you may as well go the other way. Stack three of those up, one per voice, and you have the size of one assignment. Minimise over all the assignments and you have the distance between the chords:

D(A, B) = min over bijections σ of  Σ  d( ai , σ(ai) )

Six ways, and why you only need three

Three voices can be paired with three notes in 3! = 6 ways, and the strip beside the clock shows what each one costs. From C major to G major they cost 15, 11, 9, 9, 7 and 3: the best is five times cheaper than the worst. For four-note chords there would be 24 ways, for five-note chords 120, and the brute force gets expensive fast.

It does not have to. Write both chords in ascending order round the circle and the optimal assignment is always one that preserves that order — no voice ever needs to cross another. That leaves only the three cyclic rotations to check instead of all six, and for chords of n notes only n instead of n factorial. The page checks this the hard way: over all 2,304 ordered pairs of the 48 three-note chords it can build, the cheapest of the three rotations is never once beaten by any of the six assignments.

What the distance is good for

D is a genuine metric. It is zero exactly when the two chords are the same collection of notes, it is symmetric, and it obeys the triangle inequality — all 110,592 triples of those 48 chords are checked on load. It is also blind to transposition and to inversion, which is what you want: the smoothness of a progression should not depend on what key you wrote it in.

Restrict attention to the 24 major and minor triads and a structure appears. From any one of them, exactly seven others lie within two semitones of total motion, and the list of sizes is the same for every one of the 24: two at distance 1, five at distance 2. Of those seven, exactly three move a single voice and leave the other two sitting still — one by a semitone, one by a semitone, one by a whole tone. Those three are the transformations nineteenth-century harmony leans on hardest. From C major they are C minor, E minor and A minor.

Two common tones are not, on their own, a guarantee of smoothness. Among major and minor triads they are: sharing two notes forces the third to be within two semitones, every time. Widen the collection to include diminished and augmented triads and it fails badly — C diminished and F♯ diminished share two notes and are six semitones apart, the furthest anything can be. The guarantee is a fact about consonant triads, not about common tones.

What to try

Leave the chords at C major and G major and drag the assignment slider from one end to the other: watch the arrows uncross as the total falls to 3. Now set the second chord to A minor. The best is 2, and two of the three voices do not move at all. Then set the second chord to F♯ major, a tritone away: the best of the six is 6, the worst is 18, and even the best is as far apart as two chords can be — there is no smooth way to make that move. Finally set both qualities to augmented and sweep the second root — the distance repeats every four semitones, because an augmented triad is its own transposition.
