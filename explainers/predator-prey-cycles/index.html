Two species, four rules. Prey breed on their own and get eaten. Predators starve on their own and breed in proportion to how much they catch. Write that down and you get the oldest model in mathematical ecology:

prey' = α·prey − β·prey·pred pred' = δ·prey·pred − γ·pred

Every term is the obvious one. There is no carrying capacity, no age structure, no season, no space. And yet the behaviour it produces is not obvious at all.

The loops never close in

Click anywhere in the phase plane. The trajectory is a closed loop, and it returns exactly to where you started — not approximately, not eventually, but on every single circuit. Click somewhere else and you get a different loop nested inside or outside the first. There is no attractor. The system has no preferred cycle; it just keeps whatever cycle you gave it.

That is because the model conserves a quantity, the way a frictionless pendulum conserves energy:

V = δ·prey − γ·ln(prey) + β·pred − α·ln(pred)

V is constant along every trajectory, and its level sets are the loops you see. The equilibrium in the middle, marked with a cross, is where V is smallest. Start exactly there and nothing ever happens. Start one step away and you orbit it forever at a fixed distance, which is what physicists call neutral stability: not attracting, not repelling. It is also the model's biggest weakness, because real populations do settle down, and the fix — adding a carrying capacity — turns the loops into inward spirals.

The average is the equilibrium

Here is the part worth the price of admission. Take any loop, however wild, and average the prey population over one full cycle. You do not get something in the middle of the swing. You get exactly γ/δ — the equilibrium value — and the predator average is exactly α/β. The dashed lines on the time-series panel are those averages, and they do not move when you change which loop you are on. A population that spends most of its time near the bottom and briefly explodes has the same mean as one that barely wobbles.

Notice what the averages are made of. The mean prey population depends only on γ and δ — the predator's death rate and hunting efficiency. Nothing about how fast prey breed appears in it at all. Symmetrically, the mean predator population depends only on the prey's parameters. Each species' long-run abundance is set entirely by the other one's biology.

Why spraying backfires

Now pull the culling slider. It adds the same extra death rate to both species — an insecticide that kills the pest and its natural enemy alike, or a net that catches both fish. The prey equation loses some α and the predator equation gains some γ, so the averages move to

average prey = (γ + h) / δ average predator = (α − h) / β

The average pest population goes up. Not transiently — in the long-run mean. You have removed the thing that was holding it down faster than you removed the pest itself, and the conserved structure converts that into a permanently higher average. Vito Volterra derived this in the 1920s to explain why fishing in the Adriatic had fallen during the First World War and predatory fish had become more common: less fishing meant more sharks, and more sharks meant fewer of the fish anyone wanted. The same argument, run forwards, is the standard cautionary tale about broad-spectrum pesticides.

What to try

Set the culling slider to 0.3 and watch the average-prey readout climb from 4 to 7 while the predators fall from 2.75 to 2. At the top of the slider, 0.6, the prey average has reached 10 — two and a half times where it started — and the predators are down to 1.25. Push culling past α itself and the predator average would hit zero: the enemy is wiped out, the model stops meaning anything, and that is the honest boundary of the argument.

And click far out in the top-right of the phase plane. The loop that comes back is enormous: prey crash to a fraction of one and recover to dozens. The averages are unchanged.
