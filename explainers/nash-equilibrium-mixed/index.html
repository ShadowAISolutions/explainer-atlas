Your Own Payoffs Don't Set Your Own Odds

Matching pennies has no safe move. If you always show heads, I show heads and take your coin; if you always show tails, I follow you there. The only way to stop being read is to flip, and the question the picture answers is what odds to flip at.

The square holds every pair of odds. Sideways is how often the row player picks its first action, upwards is how often the column player picks its. The red path marks the row player's best reply to each column mix, and the blue path marks the column player's best reply to each row mix. Both are staircases, because against any fixed opponent mix one pure action is strictly better than the other until a single crossover point, where the two are exactly equal and every mix is as good as any other. Equilibrium is where the two staircases meet: each side is already replying as well as it can to what the other is doing.

The condition that fixes the odds

Look at where the red staircase turns. It turns at the column mix that makes the row player's two actions pay exactly the same. That is the whole mechanism. The row player is only willing to randomise if randomising costs nothing, and it costs nothing only when both actions pay alike, so in equilibrium the column player must be mixing at precisely the odds that make its opponent indifferent.

q* solves q(a11 - a12) + a12 = q(a21 - a22) + a22 p* solves p(b11 - b21) + b21 = p(b12 - b22) + b22

Read the two lines carefully. The column player's odds q* are built entirely from a, the row player's payoffs. The row player's odds p* are built entirely from b, the column player's. Neither player's own numbers appear anywhere in their own answer. Drag Row's own payoff and you will see the red staircase slide up or down while the vertical line through the crossing does not move a pixel; drag Column's own payoff and the opposite happens. Your payoffs do not set your odds. They set your opponent's.

What the crossing is not

It is tempting to read the crossing as the only place the two paths touch. In matching pennies it is. Switch to battle of the sexes or hawk and dove and count again: the staircases also touch at two corners, because those games have pure equilibria as well as the mixed one, and a corner is a meeting point too. The page asserts all three counts rather than the tidy claim. In battle of the sexes the mixed equilibrium is the worst of the three for both players, paying each two thirds of a point against the one or two points either pure agreement would pay. Randomising is what you are left with when you cannot coordinate, not something either side wants.

Three actions, same argument

Rock, paper, scissors replaces the square with a triangle, because a mix over three actions is a point in a triangle. Colour each point by which action beats it best and the triangle splits into three regions; the only mix that no single action beats is the point where all three regions meet, one third each. Nudge Row's own payoff so that rock against rock is worth something, and the meeting point slides away from the centre, still obeying the same rule.

What to try

Start on matching pennies and put the probe marker somewhere off the crossing: the deviation readout tells you how much the better-placed player can take by shifting its odds. Slide it onto the crossing and that number falls to zero, which is the definition of equilibrium and the only place it holds. Then take Row's own payoff from 1 up to 4 and watch the vertical line refuse to move.
