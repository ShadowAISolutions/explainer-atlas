The Gaps Between Numbers Are Not All the Same Size

A double is not a real number with a bit of rounding. It is one of about 2⁶⁴ specific values, spaced unevenly along the line — dense near zero, and hundreds of millions apart out where the big numbers live.

Drag the magnitude slider from 0 up to 53 and watch the gap readout. At k = 0 the spacing is about 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁶. At k = 53 it is exactly 2. Not two parts in a billion — two. Every odd integer above 2⁵³ is simply absent from the format, which is why MAX_SAFE_INTEGER + 2 and MAX_SAFE_INTEGER + 1 are the same number.

Why it is a staircase

A double stores a sign, an 11-bit exponent and a 52-bit fraction. The value is

(−1)^s × 1.f × 2^(e − 1023)

The fraction has a fixed 52 bits regardless of scale, so within any interval [2ᵏ, 2ᵏ⁺¹) — a binade — the representable values are evenly spaced 2ᵏ⁻⁵² apart. Cross into the next binade and the exponent ticks up, so the spacing doubles. That is the staircase in the lower panel: flat across each binade, one octave step at each power of two.

The payoff is that relative precision is nearly constant: about 16 decimal digits, whether you are measuring atoms or galaxies. Floating point trades absolute accuracy for scale invariance, which is almost always the right trade — and is exactly why bugs show up as relative errors that look tiny until they are compared against something absolute.

Three ways this bites

Pick 0.1 + 0.2. Neither 0.1 nor 0.2 is representable; in binary they are repeating fractions, so each is stored as the nearest double. Their sum lands one ULP above the nearest double to 0.3, and the difference is exactly 5.55 × 10⁻¹⁷ — a specific number you can print, not vagueness.

Pick small terms get absorbed and push the term count up. Adding 10⁻¹⁶ to 1 gives back exactly 1: the true sum falls between two representable doubles and rounds to the one it started at. A hundred thousand such additions still give exactly 1. Kahan summation fixes this by keeping the lost part in a second variable and feeding it back on the next iteration — same data, same hardware, recovered digits.

Pick subtraction destroys digits. Subtracting two nearly equal numbers is exactly representable — no rounding at all — and that is the problem. The leading digits cancel and the result is dominated by whatever error was already in the operands, now promoted from the sixteenth digit to the first. This is why the quadratic formula is rewritten before it is implemented.

What to try

Set k to 52 and slide the binade position across. The value changes; the gap does not budge. Then nudge k to 53 and watch the gap double in one step. Everything about floating-point arithmetic follows from that staircase.

The practical rule: never test two floats for equality, and never use a fixed tolerance like 1e-9 without knowing the magnitudes involved. At k = 40 the gap is already larger than 1e-9, so that comparison is asking whether two numbers are closer than the format can distinguish. Scale the tolerance to the values, or compare in ULPs.
