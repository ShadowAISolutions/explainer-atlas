Three Knobs, Three Different Ways to Be Wrong

A controller watches the gap between where a thing is and where it should be, and pushes. How hard it pushes is three separate decisions, and each one fixes a fault the other two cannot.

Two plants with very different habits. The same gains will not suit both.

At time zero the setpoint jumps from 0 to 1 and stays there. Everything you see after that is the system trying to catch up. The controller only ever sees one number, the error e = setpoint − output, and turns it into a push:

u = Kp·e + Ki·∫e dt − Kd·(rate of change of the output)

Proportional: fast, and permanently short

Set Ki and Kd to zero and raise Kp on the heated tank. The response gets quicker, and the final value gets closer to 1 — but never reaches it. It settles at Kp/(1 + Kp), leaving an error of exactly 1/(1 + Kp). The reason is structural: the only thing producing any push at all is the error itself, so if the error went to zero the push would go with it and the tank would cool. A proportional controller needs a permanent error to keep working.

Raising Kp shrinks that gap but never closes it, and on the motor it buys something worse. Switch plants: the motor has nothing to stop it, so a big proportional push sends it sailing past the setpoint and the error reverses. You get ringing that takes longer to die out the harder you push.

Integral: patient, and prone to overshoot

Integral action keeps a running total of the error. As long as any gap remains, the total keeps growing and the push keeps rising, so the only resting state is zero error. Add a little Ki on the tank and watch the final value climb onto 1 and stay.

The cost is memory. By the time the output reaches the setpoint, the accumulated total is large and still pushing, so the system sails past and the integral has to be unwound from the other side. Too much Ki and that unwinding never converges — the system oscillates with growing amplitude. On the motor, with the plant's own integration on top, the whole loop goes unstable once

(2 + Kd) · Kp < Ki

which is the Routh–Hurwitz condition for this loop's characteristic polynomial. It is not a rule of thumb; cross that line and the poles move into the right half-plane. Drag Ki up with Kp at 2 and Kd at 0 and the plot diverges just past Ki = 4.

Derivative: steadying, and easily fooled

Derivative action pushes against motion rather than against error, so it acts as damping. On the motor, adding Kd is what makes an aggressive Kp usable: it lets you raise the loop's speed without the ringing, and it widens the stability condition above. It is also the term that adds nothing at all in steady state, since the output has stopped moving by then.

Its weakness is that a derivative amplifies anything fast, and real measurements are noisy. Here the signal is clean, so Kd looks like a free win. On a real sensor it is the term people turn down first.

What to try

Match the grey target on each plant. On the motor you can hit it almost exactly with Kp near 4.85, Ki at zero and Kd near 1.10 — no integral needed, because a motor that coasts already holds its position without a push. On the tank the same gains leave you short, and you have to bring Ki up to close the gap, which is the whole lesson in one drag.
