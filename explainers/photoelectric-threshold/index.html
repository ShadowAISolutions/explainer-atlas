Then do the thing that feels obviously decisive: turn the brightness all the way up and guess again.

The toll at the surface

An electron inside a metal sits in a hole. Climbing out costs a fixed amount of energy called the work function, and that price is set by the metal, not by the light. On the wave picture the light's energy arrives continuously, spread across the whole plate, so a dim beam should simply take longer to accumulate enough — a pause, then electrons. On the photon picture the energy arrives in indivisible lumps, one electron absorbs one lump, and a lump smaller than the toll buys nothing however many of them turn up.

E_max = h f - W V_stop = E_max / e

The two pictures disagree about something you can settle in an afternoon. Set caesium and 650 nanometres. Caesium's toll is 2.14 electron-volts and that light carries 1.907, so the ammeter reads zero. Now take the brightness to the top of its range. The plate is receiving something like 2.5 × 1018 photons every second, and the current is still zero. Not small. Zero.

Turn the wavelength down instead. Near 579 nanometres, in the yellow, caesium starts emitting, and every further step towards the blue makes the individual electrons faster while the number of them, at fixed power, falls. That part catches people out. One watt of 200-nanometre light contains exactly half as many photons as one watt of 400-nanometre light, because each photon costs twice as much, and the page checks it: the current at 400 nanometres is exactly 2.000000 times the current at 200.

Brightness does do something, and it is precisely what the photon picture says it should: more photons, more electrons, more current, in exact proportion. What it never does is change how fast any single electron is moving. Raise the retarding voltage until the current just dies and you have measured that speed directly. For potassium at 350 nanometres it dies at 1.242406 volts, and it dies at 1.242406 volts whether the lamp is at its dimmest or five decades brighter.

The measurement that paid for a constant

Every reveal drops a point on the right-hand plot: stopping voltage against frequency. Two points from one metal are enough to fit a line, and the slope of that line is Planck's constant divided by the charge on the electron. Millikan spent a decade trying to break Einstein's equation this way and in 1916 published the best value of h then known, from exactly this plot.

The telling part is what the slope refuses to depend on. Change metals and the whole line slides sideways, because the intercept is the work function — but the slope does not budge. All seven metals here return the same slope to within a few parts in 1016. The metal sets the price of admission; the exchange rate between frequency and energy is not the metal's to set.

Two idealisations worth naming

The electrons leave with a spread of energies, taken here as uniform up to the maximum, which makes the current fall along a straight line to zero. Real distributions are not uniform. Only the point where that line reaches zero is independent of the shape, which is why the stopping voltage, and not the slope of the current, is what an experiment reports. The switch-on at threshold is drawn as a step, too; a real photocathode rounds that corner over roughly the thermal energy, and under a focused laser two photons can together do what neither can alone. That last effect is real, and it is also sixty years younger than the argument this page is about.

What to try

Caesium at 650 nanometres, guess nothing, reveal. Drag the brightness to maximum and guess again: same answer. Walk the wavelength down through 579 and watch the ammeter come alive the moment a single photon can pay. Switch to platinum and repeat the whole sweep — nothing happens anywhere in the visible, because its threshold is 219 nanometres. Then reveal four or five runs of one metal at different wavelengths and watch the fitted slope settle onto Planck's constant.
