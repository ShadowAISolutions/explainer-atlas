Verdict
—

A population has settled on one way of behaving. A few individuals turn up doing something else. Either they fade out over a few generations and the population is unchanged, or they spread and the old way is gone. A strategy is called evolutionarily stable when no rare newcomer can spread against it, and the whole of that definition is visible in the middle panel: the mutant's fitness minus the incumbent's, drawn against how common the mutant is.

That gap is a straight line, and it is worth being precise about why. Write the payoff to a player using strategy a against one using b as E(a, b). If a share x of the population is mutant, a mutant meets another mutant a fraction x of the time and an incumbent the rest of the time, so the two fitnesses are mixtures with weights x and 1 minus x. Subtract them and every term is either constant or proportional to x. The line is fixed by its ends.

gap(x) = (1−x)·[E(q,p) − E(p,p)] + x·[E(q,q) − E(p,q)]

The left end asks whether a lone mutant, surrounded entirely by incumbents, does better than an incumbent does. The right end asks something quite different: in a world made of mutants, would a single incumbent be doing better? Those are the two conditions in the textbook definition, and they are the two endpoints of one line. Stability just means the line sits below zero for small x.

Being unbeatable without being the best reply to yourself

Set the game to Hawk and Dove and press the button that moves the incumbent to the stable strategy. The left end of the line drops to exactly zero. That is not a coincidence or a rounding artefact. At the mix where hawk and dove earn the same, every strategy earns the same, because any mix of two equally good options is just as good. A lone mutant is doing precisely as well as the residents. It has no way in.

So the first condition gives nothing, and the whole weight falls on the second. It comes out beautifully: the incumbent's advantage in a field of mutants is exactly C·(p* − q)² / 2, a square, so it is positive for every mutant that differs at all. The further the newcomers stray, the worse they do — against each other. The stable mix is not the best answer to itself. It is merely the only thing that cannot be improved on from the outside, which turns out to be the property that matters.

Where Nash is not enough

Switch to the second game. Two habits pay identically against the resident habit, so neither can be undercut and both are Nash equilibria. But one of them copes with meeting itself and the other does not. Start from the one that does not and the left end of the line is zero again — and the right end is positive. The mutants cannot get in quickly, but they are never pushed back either, and drift is enough: the bottom panel shows one per cent crawling, then turning over the whole population inside five hundred generations. Every stable strategy is a Nash equilibrium; this is a Nash equilibrium that is not stable.

What to try

Leave the incumbent at the stable mix and sweep the mutant slider from end to end: the left endpoint never moves off zero, and the right endpoint traces a parabola. Then drag the incumbent away from the mix by a little and the left endpoint lifts off zero — now invasion is easy, and the population is dragged back toward V / C from wherever you put it. Finally raise V above C. The cost of fighting no longer exceeds the prize, V / C is larger than one and is not a share of anything, and the stable answer is no longer a mix at all: it is hawk, every time.
