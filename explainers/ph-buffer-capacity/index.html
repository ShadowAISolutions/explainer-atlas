The Flat Part of the Curve

Drip sodium hydroxide into a weak acid and, for a long while, almost nothing happens to the pH. Then it happens all at once. The flat part has a width, a height, and a closed form.

Start at zero added base. The beaker holds 50 mL of a weak acid at 0.10 molar, and its pH sits near 2.9 — acidic, but nowhere near the pH 1 you would get from a strong acid at the same concentration. Most of the acid is sitting there intact, undissociated, holding its proton.

Now start adding base. Each hydroxide ion you add finds a proton and takes it, converting one molecule of acid into one of its conjugate base. You might expect the pH to climb steadily. It does not. From about 5 mL to about 45 mL the curve is nearly flat, rising only about two units across forty millilitres. Then, between 49 and 51 mL, it leaps by more than four.

Why the flat part is flat

At any moment the mixture contains both the acid HA and its conjugate base A⁻, and the equilibrium between them pins the hydrogen ion concentration to their ratio:

pH = pKa + log₁₀( [A⁻] / [HA] )

That is the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation, and the logarithm is the whole story. To move the pH by one unit you must change the ratio by a factor of ten. Near the middle of the titration the ratio is close to one, so you have to convert an enormous amount of acid to shift it tenfold. Every drop you add is absorbed by that conversion instead of showing up as free hydrogen ion. The mixture is not resisting you chemically; it is resisting you logarithmically.

Drag the pKa slider and watch the flat region slide vertically without changing shape. The acid's strength sets the height of the plateau. It does not set its width.

Capacity has a closed form

Buffer capacity β is defined as how much base you must add to move the pH by one unit. For a single weak acid at total concentration C it works out to

β = ln(10) · C · Ka[H⁺] / (Ka + [H⁺])²

which is maximised exactly when [H⁺] equals Ka — that is, at pH = pKa, the half-equivalence point, where half the acid has been converted and the two forms are present in equal amounts. Put that back in and the peak value is simply

βmax = ln(10) · C / 4 ≈ 0.576 · C

One pH unit away from the pKa, in either direction, capacity has fallen to 40/121 of its peak — about a third. That ratio is exact and independent of everything: the concentration, the acid, the temperature of the room. It is why chemists quote a buffer's useful range as pKa ± 1 and stop there. The shaded band on the chart is that range.

Where it gives way

At 50 mL you have added exactly enough base to convert all the acid. The beaker now holds the conjugate base and nothing else, and a conjugate base is itself weakly basic — so the equivalence point of a weak acid titration is not pH 7. It is above it, near 8.7 for acetic acid, and the readout will show you it moves up as the acid gets weaker. Past that point the excess hydroxide simply accumulates and the curve flattens again, this time because it is tracking a strong base being diluted.

What to try

Set the concentration to 0.01 M and run the titration again. The plateau sits at the same height — pKa does not care how much acid you have — but it is ten times shallower and the jump at the end is smaller and less sharp. Capacity scales with concentration; position does not.

Then push the pKa to 9.25, ammonium's value, and watch the plateau climb until it collides with the equivalence jump. A very weak acid barely has a titration curve at all: the buffer region and the endpoint run into each other, which is exactly why you cannot titrate a very weak acid accurately in water.
