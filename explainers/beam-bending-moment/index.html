The beam opens in failure. A 75 by 150 millimetre softwood joist over four metres, carrying three kilonewtons in the middle, sags 17.2 millimetres where 11.1 is allowed, so the curve dips below the dashed line. There are four ways to fix it and they are wildly unequal in price. The point of this page is to find out which.

Two cubes and a division

Only one formula is doing any work here. A simply supported beam with a single load in the middle sags by the load times the cube of the span, divided by forty-eight times the stiffness times the section's second moment of area — and for a rectangle that second moment is the width times the cube of the depth, over twelve.

sag = P L³ / (48 E I), I = b h³ / 12

Put those together and the four knobs are not comparable. Load and width and stiffness each appear once: double any of them and the sag halves or doubles. Span and depth each appear cubed: double the depth and the sag falls by a factor of eight, double the span and it rises by a factor of eight, triple the span and it rises by twenty-seven. Nothing on this page is subtle. It is a single expression with two cubes in it, and every piece of intuition about beams follows from which letters got cubed.

Why joists are tall and thin

The default beam needs its sag cut by a factor of 1.55. Going deeper, that is a cube root: 175 millimetres of depth does it, twenty-five millimetres more timber in one direction, about seventeen per cent more material. Going wider, it is a straight division: the width has to rise from 75 millimetres to 120, which is sixty per cent more material for the same result. Same fix, three and a half times the cost.

The clean way to state this is at constant material. Hold the cross-sectional area fixed and the sag falls as the square of the depth — the self-test checks that by squashing each test section into six different shapes of identical area and confirming that sag times depth squared never budges. That is the whole argument for a joist being a tall thin rectangle stood on edge, and for the same plank laid flat being hopeless. Lay the default section down instead of standing it up and the sag goes from 17 millimetres to 69.

Sag, not strength

The peak bending stress at the default settings is 10.7 megapascals, comfortably inside what even a low grade of structural softwood carries. The beam is in no danger at all of breaking. It fails because it is bouncy, and a floor that bounces is a floor people complain about. That is why the dashed line here is span over 360 rather than anything to do with material strength — for floor beams the serviceability limit normally binds long before the strength limit does, and the beam you buy is the one that stops the floor moving.

Stress and sag also disagree about which knob matters. Stress goes as 3 P L over 2 b h² — depth squared, not cubed. So deepening a beam buys stiffness faster than it buys strength, and the two limits cross over as spans get longer. Watch both readouts as you drag the span: the share of the limit climbs as the cube while the stress climbs only linearly.

What to try

Get the beam to pass on depth alone, then undo it and get it to pass on width alone, and compare the two sections drawn in the corner. Then leave the section as it started and switch the material to steel: the sag drops to 0.95 millimetres, eighteen times better, because stiffness enters once like width. Finally set the span to 8 metres and watch every fix stop working at once.
