Nothing Mystical Happens At Pi

Multiplying by a complex number of length one is a turn, and nothing else. Build the turn out of many tiny multiplications and the famous identity stops being a magic trick.

Set the angle to 180 degrees and cut it into three steps. The terracotta path starts at 1, takes three equal turns and lands a long way outside the frame — nowhere near the ring that marks where half a turn should end. Now push the step count up. The polygon shortens, curls inward, and by about forty steps its last vertex is sitting inside the ring. Nothing was smoothed or fitted to make that happen. The same multiplication was simply done more times, each time by less.

A turn is a multiplication

Every complex number has a length and a direction, and multiplying two of them multiplies the lengths and adds the directions. That is the whole content of complex multiplication. A number whose length is exactly one therefore does nothing at all to length and everything to direction: multiplying by it is a pure turn. The circle in the picture is the set of all such numbers, and “rotate by an angle” and “multiply by the point at that angle on the circle” are the same instruction.

Where e comes in

Cut the turn into n equal pieces. Each piece ought to be a multiplication by something barely off 1, and the natural guess is 1 + iθ/n: keep the length, nudge sideways by a fraction of the way round.

e^(iθ) = the limit of (1 + iθ/n)^n as n grows

The guess is slightly wrong, and the picture shows exactly how. A sideways nudge from a point on the circle leaves the circle, because the tangent runs outside it. Every vertex of the terracotta polygon therefore sits a little too far out, and the finished polygon is too long by about θ²/2n — which is why the polygon length readout reaches 1.0000 only in the limit. Its angle falls short by about θ³/3n², for the same reason in reverse: chords cut corners. One error dies as 1/n and the other as 1/n², which is why forty steps already looks like a circle while three does not.

That limit is the definition of the exponential, and putting θ = π into it makes the turn a half turn, which sends 1 to −1. The famous identity is not a coincidence between three celebrated constants. It is the sentence “half a turn is a reflection”, written in the notation that turns turning into multiplying.

The second route

The green path is the Taylor series, added one power at a time: 1, then iθ, then (iθ)²/2, and onward. Each term is a quarter turn from the one before and shorter by a factor of θ/k, so the path spirals inward and closes on the same point the polygon is crawling towards. At a half turn the early terms are larger than 1 — the third alone is nearly 5 — so with six of them the path swings right out of the frame and comes back, landing near 0.12 + 0.52i, which is still nowhere near −1. Each further term shrinks the gap by roughly θ/k, so the error falls off a cliff once k passes θ.

The self-test pins that down rather than asserting it loosely: across five angles and every cut point from four terms to eighteen, the distance from the truncated sum to the true point is never larger than the size of the very next term dropped, and never smaller than sixty per cent of it. Two constructions that share no machinery — repeated multiplication and an infinite sum — land on the same point, and neither one had to be told what a cosine is.

What to try

Set the angle to 90 degrees and the terms to 3. The series has only reached −0.23 + 1.57i, well outside the circle, and needs four more terms before it is within a hundredth of i. Then set the angle to 360 degrees and leave the steps at three: the polygon is more than twelve times too long and runs clean off the frame without completing a single turn. Drag the step count from there and watch it fall back through the circle and settle on 1, which is where a whole turn has to end.
