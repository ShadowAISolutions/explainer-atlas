Two beakers, two metals, a wire and a salt bridge. The metal on the left dissolves, its ions going into solution; the ions on the right plate out onto the metal there. The electrons that transaction requires travel through the wire, and the voltmeter measures how badly they want to make the trip.

That eagerness has two parts. One is fixed by which metals you chose — the standard potential, the voltage the cell would show if both solutions were at one mole per litre. The other depends on how much of each ion is actually present, and it is the part people forget.

E = E° − (RT / nF) ln Q, Q = [ion made] / [ion used up]

Drag the cathode ion down and the voltage climbs: the cell is hungrier when the thing it wants is scarce, which is backwards from most intuitions until you remember that Q is a ratio and the scarce ion is on the bottom. At 25 °C, every factor of ten in that ratio is worth 59.2 millivolts to a cell that moves one electron, and half of that, 29.6 millivolts, to one that moves two. Switch to the one-electron cell and watch the same slider do twice as much.

Voltage is a slope

Turn on the free-energy curve. The green line is the total Gibbs free energy of everything in both beakers, plotted against how far the reaction has run from where it stands now. It falls, because that is what spontaneous means, and it reaches a minimum.

The voltage is not some separate fact about the cell. It is the steepness of that curve.

E = −(1 / nF) × dG/dξ

Steep curve, high voltage. Flat curve, no voltage. This is worth pausing on, because it explains the thing that makes no sense otherwise: a dead cell still contains both metals and both solutions, in quantity. Nothing has been used up in the sense of being gone. What has gone is the slope. The system has slid to the bottom of its own free-energy valley, and at the bottom there is no downhill direction left to push electrons around a circuit.

Where the bottom is

At the minimum, Q has climbed until it equals the equilibrium constant K, and K is fixed by the standard potential alone.

K = exp( nF E° / RT )

Because E° sits inside an exponential, ordinary cell voltages give preposterous equilibrium constants. The Daniell cell's standard potential is about 1.10 volts, which puts K near 1037. Read the last box: starting from 0.1 M copper, the equilibrium sits near 5 × 10−39 M, which is one copper ion per few hundred cubic kilometres of solution. A zinc-copper cell never gets anywhere near that. It stops because the copper physically runs out first, and even the mildest cell offered here, the one-electron silver one, still consumes better than 99.99 per cent of its silver before the voltage would reach zero.

The concentration cell is the honest demonstration. Same metal on both sides, so E° is exactly zero, K is exactly one, and equilibrium is simply the point where the two solutions have the same concentration. Choose it and the minimum of the free-energy curve is somewhere you can actually see, with the voltage crossing zero directly above it. That cell is driven by nothing but the concentration difference, and it dies when the difference does.

What to try

Start with the Daniell cell and slide the cathode ion from 1 M down to a micromolar: the voltage moves by less than 200 millivolts across six orders of magnitude, which is why batteries hold a nearly flat voltage almost until the moment they quit. Then switch to the concentration cell, set the two concentrations equal, and watch the voltmeter read zero with both beakers visibly full. Finally, make the cathode ion the more concentrated of the two and then the less, and see the voltage change sign as the cell runs the other way.
