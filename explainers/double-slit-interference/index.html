Two Terms, One Pattern

The stripes on the screen come from how far apart the slits are. Their fading brightness comes from how wide each slit is. Build the pattern one part at a time and the two stop being the same thing.

Step through the four settings at the top in order. One slit gives a single broad blob. Two slits of no width at all give evenly spaced stripes of equal brightness, marching off to the edge of the screen. Two real slits give those same stripes, in the same places, with the blob laid over them as a brightness curve. The third picture is the first two multiplied together, and that is the whole of the double-slit pattern.

Which knob moves which part

Angles are measured by the sine of the deflection. Light from adjacent slits arrives in step when the extra distance it travels is a whole number of wavelengths, which puts the bright fringes at

fringe maxima: d sin(theta) = m * lambda envelope zeros: a sin(theta) = m * lambda fringe spacing on a screen at L: approximately lambda * L / d

Two facts fall out of that. The fringe positions depend on the separation and not at all on the width, so widening the slits does not move a single stripe. And the envelope's first dark ring depends on the width and not at all on the separation. Slide the separation and watch only the stripe spacing change; slide the width and watch only the brightness curve breathe, while every stripe stays exactly where it was.

Missing orders

When the separation is an exact whole-number multiple of the width, a fringe maximum lands precisely on an envelope zero and that stripe disappears. Set the separation to 24 micrometres and the width to 8, and the third stripe on each side is simply not there — the interference says bright, the diffraction says dark, and dark wins because the product of anything with zero is zero. The readout names the missing order whenever the ratio is a whole number.

The same product explains the last part. Adding more slits leaves every maximum exactly where the two-slit pattern put it, because they satisfy the same condition, but between each pair of maxima there are now N−1 directions where the slits cancel perfectly and N−2 feeble leftovers. The peaks get narrower and brighter while the light between them drains away. That is a diffraction grating, and it is why a grating can separate two wavelengths a prism cannot.

What to try

Put the separation at 25 micrometres and sweep the wavelength from 400 to 700. The whole pattern stretches by three quarters, which is the entire principle behind a spectrometer. Then set the width equal to the separation: the two slits are now touching, and the pattern collapses to exactly the single-slit pattern of a slit twice as wide — an identity the page checks to the last bit of double precision, because a model that gets that case wrong is wrong everywhere.
