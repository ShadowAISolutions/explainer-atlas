The Bootstrap Is Not A Coverage Machine

Resampling your own data gives you an interval for any statistic at all, with no formula and no distribution theory. Race it against the textbook formula on the one statistic the textbook covers, and it loses — which tells you exactly what the bootstrap is really for.

The textbook has a formula for two of these. The bootstrap does not care which.

The truth is known here, which is the only reason coverage can be measured at all.

You have twenty-five numbers and you want to know how far the mean of them could be from the mean of whatever produced them. The textbook answer needs a distribution, a standard error and a table of critical values. The bootstrap answer needs none of those. Treat the sample as if it were the population, draw twenty-five numbers from it with replacement, take the mean of those, and do it four hundred times. The middle 95 per cent of the answers is your interval. No algebra anywhere.

It is a genuinely remarkable idea and it works on anything: the median, the interquartile range, a trimmed mean, the ratio of two things, the output of a model you cannot differentiate. Switch statistic and the bootstrap carries on without comment while the formula column goes blank. That is what the bootstrap is for.

Now watch it lose the race

On normal data with twenty-five points, asked for 95 per cent, the textbook interval contains the true mean 94.6 per cent of the time and the bootstrap interval contains it 92.5 per cent of the time, measured over three thousand fresh samples. One Monte Carlo standard error at that many samples is half a point, so the shortfall is five of them: the bootstrap is not unlucky and it is not noisy, it is systematically short. Drag sample size down to ten and it falls to 88 per cent. Drag it up to a hundred and twenty and it is still only 94.

Two things cause it, and both are exact. The bootstrap resamples from the sample, so the spread it sees is the sample's own spread with the divisor n rather than n minus one. That makes its standard error smaller than the textbook one by a factor of

sqrt((n - 1) / n) = 0.9798 at n = 25

which is 2.0 per cent, every time, no matter how many resamples you take. And the percentile bootstrap cuts its interval at the quantiles of what it saw, which behave like the quantiles of a normal distribution, while the formula uses Student's t: 1.960 against 2.064 at twenty-four degrees of freedom, another 5.0 per cent. Multiply the two and you predict a bootstrap interval at 0.9305 of the formula's width. The measured figure over nine hundred samples is 0.9241. The whole of the shortfall is accounted for before any data are drawn.

The rescue that is not one

The obvious defence is that the formula only wins because the data really are normal, and that the bootstrap comes into its own when they are not. Select the Pareto population and watch. Coverage falls to about 83 per cent — for both of them, with the bootstrap a point behind. On the lognormal it is 86 per cent for the bootstrap and 88 for the formula. The heavy tail breaks the bootstrap exactly as it breaks the formula, and for the same reason: a sample of twenty-five from a distribution whose extreme values matter has probably not seen one yet, and resampling from it cannot conjure the tail it never drew. Press show me where both fail to land on that setting directly.

What it is actually worth

The bootstrap does not buy accuracy. It buys scope. It gives you an interval where there was no interval to be had, and that interval is about as trustworthy as a formula-based one would have been if a formula had existed: roughly right on well-behaved data, optimistic on small samples, and badly wrong on heavy tails. Knowing the size of each of those failures is the difference between using the bootstrap and believing it.

The fixes exist and they are not free. Using the quantiles the other way round gives the basic interval, correcting for skew and bias gives the accelerated one, and bootstrapping a studentised quantity gets closest of all at the cost of a bootstrap inside every bootstrap. All of them sharpen the same tool. None of them makes a sample of twenty-five know about a tail it has never seen.

What to try

Leave everything at the defaults and drag resamples from 50 upward. Coverage climbs from about 90 to about 92 per cent over the first couple of hundred, because at sixty draws the 2.5 per cent cut falls between the second and third smallest of them and is itself a coarse guess. Past two hundred it stops moving: the histogram keeps smoothing and the coverage does not improve. Then set the statistic to the median on normal data. The formula column empties, and the bootstrap interval is about a quarter wider than the mean's, which is the square root of pi over two that the theory predicts, and which you would have had no way to price at all without resampling.
