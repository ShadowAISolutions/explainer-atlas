The Far End of the Fin Is Cold

A longer cooling fin always throws off more heat. It also always throws off less heat per millimetre of metal, and past a certain point the extra length is very nearly free real estate for nothing.

Both fins are aluminium, one millimetre thick, forty millimetres wide, sitting in the same moving air with their bases fifty degrees above ambient. Fin B is two and a quarter times as long as fin A. It dissipates about an eighth more heat. Look at the shading and you can see where the extra metal went: the far third of B sits within seven degrees of the air, and metal at air temperature transfers nothing.

Why the tip goes cold

Heat enters the fin at the base and travels along it by conduction, leaking out sideways into the air the whole way. Steady state balances the two. For a fin of cross-section Ac and perimeter P, writing θ for the temperature rise above ambient:

k Ac d²θ/dx² = h P θ   →   θ(x) = θb · cosh(m(L − x)) / cosh(mL),   m = √(hP / kAc)

The whole of the fin's behaviour is in the single number mL. It is the fin's length measured in units of how far heat can travel along that metal before the air takes it. The tip rise is θb/cosh(mL): at mL = 1 the tip is still at 65 percent of the base rise, at mL = 3 it is at 10 percent, and at mL = 5 it is at 1.3 percent. Everything beyond about three is a handle, not a heat sink.

Efficiency, and what it is efficiency against

The most a fin of that surface area could possibly do is sit entirely at base temperature, giving h P L θb. Fin efficiency is the fraction of that it actually achieves, and it collapses to a function of mL alone:

η = q / (h P L θb) = tanh(mL) / mL

That is a falling curve: 1 at zero length, 0.762 at mL = 1, 0.482 at 2, 0.332 at 3. Meanwhile the heat itself, q = √(hPkAc) θb tanh(mL), is a rising curve that flattens. Both are true at once, and the interesting thing is where they cross over into pointlessness. Doubling a fin from mL = 1 to mL = 2 buys 26.6 percent more heat. Doubling from 3 to 6 buys 0.50 percent. Doubling from 4 to 8 buys 0.067 percent.

So how long should it be?

Not "as long as fits". Fix the volume of metal instead and ask where to put it, and a genuine optimum appears: for a straight rectangular fin the best dissipation per unit volume is at mL = 1.4192, which is the number the page recomputes from the model on load. Below it you are wasting the base area you have; above it you are wasting metal. Real heat sinks sit near there, which is why they are made of many short fins rather than a few tall ones — and why the fix for an underperforming sink is usually more fins, thicker fins, or more airflow, and almost never longer fins.

What to try

Drag fin B out to 120 mm and watch the verdict line: the metal doubles again and the heat does not move. Now drop the conductivity to 45, the value for steel. The same lengths that were fine in aluminium are suddenly far past useful, because m went up by more than a factor of two — a steel fin has to be much shorter to stay efficient. Finally take the air-side coefficient down to 10, the value for still air, and the opposite happens: with the air barely taking heat away, the same 120 mm fin goes from 24 percent efficient to 69 percent. It also dissipates a quarter as much heat as it did under the fan. Efficiency is not a virtue on its own. A fin can be highly efficient and still be dissipating almost nothing.
