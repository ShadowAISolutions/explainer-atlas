The chance a move comes out the opposite of what was intended. Trembling hands.

The payoffs are the standard ones. If you both cooperate you get 3 each. If you both defect you get 1 each. If one defects and the other cooperates, the defector gets 5 and the cooperator gets nothing.

they cooperate they defect you cooperate 3, 3 0, 5 you defect 5, 0 1, 1

One round has only one answer

Look down either column. If they cooperate, you get 5 by defecting and 3 by cooperating. If they defect, you get 1 by defecting and 0 by cooperating. Defection is better in both cases, so it does not matter what you believe about the other player — defecting dominates. Both of you reason this way, both defect, and you each get 1 when you could each have had 3. That is the whole dilemma, and in a single round there is no way out of it.

Repetition changes the arithmetic

Now suppose the game continues with probability δ after each round. Against an opponent who cooperates until you cross them, cooperating forever is worth 3/(1−δ). Defecting once and then living with mutual defection is worth 5 + δ·1/(1−δ). Set those equal and the break-even discount is

δ* = (T − R) / (T − P) = (5 − 3) / (5 − 1) = 0.5

Above a fifty percent chance of another round, cooperating is strictly better — not out of goodwill but because the future is worth more than the one-off gain. Below it, defect. Nothing about the payoffs changed; only the shadow of the future did.

What actually wins

Run the tournament with noise at zero and tit for tat does well without ever beating anyone. It never can. The score gap in any match is exactly five times the difference in unilateral defections — the rounds where one player defects and the other does not — and tit for tat plays whatever you played last round, so it can never defect alone more often than you did. It wins tournaments by refusing to lose badly, which is a different thing from winning matches. Always-defect beats it head to head by exactly the one point it steals in round one, and then finishes far below it overall, because against other defectors it grinds out 1 a round while the cooperative strategies are collecting 3.

Note what that argument does not rest on. Opening nicely is not enough on its own: grim trigger is just as nice, and against the coin flip it takes 329 points to 74, because once a random defection trips it, it defects for the rest of the match and collects 5 every time the coin comes up cooperate. Mirroring is the property that caps a strategy at a draw; niceness only decides who draws first blood.

Then turn the noise up

Set noise to 0.01 — one move in a hundred comes out wrong. Tit for tat against itself falls from 3.00 to about 2.42, because a single slip starts an echo: I retaliate, you retaliate for my retaliation, and the two of us alternate recriminations until another slip happens to break the pattern. Grim trigger does far worse, around 1.52, since one accident is permanent.

Pavlov — repeat your last move if it worked, switch if it did not — holds around 2.95, because after mutual accidental defection both players switch back to cooperating simultaneously. The lesson that survives from the tournament literature is not "be nice"; it is that a strategy needs a way back. Try writing one.

What to try

Write return round % 2 === 0; and watch a perfectly predictable alternator get exploited by anything that notices. Then try a forgiving retaliator: defect if they defected last round and the round before. It loses a little to pure defectors and gains a lot under noise.

Then set rounds to 1 and run it. Every strategy that opens nicely scores 0 against every defector, and the ranking inverts completely. One round really does have only one answer.
