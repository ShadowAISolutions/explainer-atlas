The seafloor is young in the middle of an ocean and old at its edges, and the pattern is almost embarrassingly orderly. New crust is made along a ridge, the two flanks carry it away in opposite directions, and nothing much happens to it afterwards. So the distance from the ridge to a patch of seafloor, divided by the speed at which the flank moves, is that patch's age.

age = distance from the ridge ÷ half-spreading rate

The half-rate is the speed of one flank. The two plates separate at twice that, which is why the literature is full of both numbers and why it pays to check which one is meant. Convenient accident of units: one millimetre per year is exactly one kilometre per million years, so a 30 mm/yr half-rate puts 3-million-year-old crust 90 kilometres out.

Where the clock came from

Nobody could read this clock until the magnetic stripes were explained. The Earth's magnetic field reverses, at intervals of tens of thousands to millions of years, and basalt freezing at a ridge locks in whichever direction the field had at the time. The result is a barcode, laid down at the ridge and carried outward symmetrically, so the same sequence of stripes appears on both flanks as mirror images. Switch the map to magnetic polarity and the barcode is the whole picture.

That gives the rate without knowing anything about the crust itself. The boundary between the Brunhes and Matuyama chrons is dated at about 773,000 years ago. Find that boundary on the map, measure how far out it sits, divide, and you have the half-rate. Do it for every boundary at once and the fit is the reading shown as rate from the stripes. It agrees with the rate you set, because that is the arithmetic the map was built from — but it is the arithmetic that turned seafloor spreading from a proposal into a measurement.

When the pace changes

Ridges do not spread at a fixed rate forever. Set the two half-rate sliders apart and watch the profile: the age-against-distance line has a kink, and the stripes change width at the same place. Wide stripes mean fast spreading, because more crust was made between one reversal and the next. Reading a rate off a stretch of seafloor that straddles the changeover gives an average that belongs to neither era — the fit has to be done on each limb separately, which is why real studies pick a chron interval and stay inside it.

Two other things the map knows

Turn on the transform faults. The ridge breaks into segments offset sideways from one another, and because each segment feeds only its own strip of ocean, the ages jump across the fault instead of varying smoothly. The offset in age is exactly the offset in distance divided by the rate. This is why fracture zones show up so sharply on real age maps.

Then switch to water depth. Fresh crust at a ridge is hot and buoyant, sits high, and sinks as it cools and contracts. Working out how far it sinks means integrating the contraction through a cooling column of rock, which sounds like it should produce something complicated. It produces the square root of age.

depth ≈ 2500 m + 355 m × √(age in Ma)

Old seafloor is deep because it is old, not because it started low. The relationship is good out to roughly 80 million years, after which real seafloor stops sinking as fast as a plain cooling half-space says it should.

What to try

Set the changeover to zero so the ridge has one steady rate, put the crosshair a long way out, and check that age really is distance over rate. Then set the recent half-rate to 20 and the older one to 90 and look at the magnetic map: the narrow stripes near the ridge and the wide ones beyond the kink are what a slowdown looks like. Finally, drag the crosshair straight across the ridge and watch the age readout fall to zero and climb again by the same amounts — the two flanks are mirror images, which is the observation the whole theory rests on.
